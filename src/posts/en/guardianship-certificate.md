A guardianship certificate is one of those documents nobody thinks about until an institution demands it: a bank holding a deceased father's account with minor heirs, a housing authority transferring property that includes a child's inheritance share, an embassy processing a minor's immigration case, or a school or hospital needing a legally authorised decision-maker. This is what it is and how it is obtained.

## Guardian of person vs guardian of property

Under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, the Guardian Court can appoint a **guardian of the person** (who cares for and decides for the child) and a **guardian of property** (who manages the minor's assets). The father is the natural guardian; after him, the question opens up. A mother raising her children after her husband's death typically needs formal appointment as guardian of property to deal with the children's inherited shares — her custody of them is not, by itself, authority over their property in the eyes of banks and registrars.

## When you need the certificate

The recurring situations: releasing a minor's share from a deceased parent's bank account, provident fund or insurance; selling, transferring or mortgaging property in which a minor holds a share; pursuing or settling litigation on a minor's behalf; immigration and visa processes requiring proof of legal guardianship; and travel documents where one parent is absent, deceased or withholding cooperation.

## The process

A petition is filed before the Guardian Judge of the district where the minor ordinarily resides, stating the minor's particulars, assets, the relationship of the petitioner, and why appointment serves the minor's welfare. Notice goes to relatives whose interest the law recognises — typically the paternal side where a mother applies — and a general citation is published. If no genuine contest emerges, evidence is brief: the petitioner proves relationship, the minor's assets, and her fitness. Courts commonly appoint within two to four months in uncontested matters.

The court may require the guardian of property to furnish a **surety bond** proportionate to the assets, and to file periodic **accounts** of the minor's property — protections for the child, not obstacles.

## The critical restriction: selling a minor's property

Appointment does not confer a free hand. A guardian **cannot sell, mortgage, gift or lease the minor's immovable property without the Guardian Court's prior permission**, and permission is granted only where the transaction is manifestly for the minor's benefit — education, medical need, or a plainly advantageous sale with proceeds secured for the minor. A sale of a minor's share without such permission is voidable at the minor's instance on attaining majority — which is precisely how many old property transactions unravel decades later. Anyone *buying* property from an estate with minor heirs should demand the guardianship certificate and the sale-permission order; their absence is a title defect.

## Practical notes

Apply early — institutions will not wait, and the citation period sets the minimum timeline. Be complete about assets in the petition; discovering assets later means amendment applications. Where the extended family supports the arrangement, their written no-objection speeds everything. And where a dispute is brewing — an uncle asserting control over the children's inheritance, for instance — the guardianship petition is not paperwork but the battlefield itself: the certificate determines who controls the minor's estate until majority. In such cases, treat it with the seriousness of the property litigation it really is.
