A couple married for fifteen years, with three children and a B-form to prove it, walks into a NADRA office or an embassy and discovers the marriage does not exist on paper: the nikah was performed in the village, the nikah khwan took the forms away, and no one ever checked that they reached the Union Council. The marriage is real; the record is not. Pakistani law separates those two questions cleanly, and fixing the second one is almost always possible.

## Validity and registration are different questions

A nikah is valid under Muslim law when its essentials are met — offer and acceptance in one sitting, witnesses, and no legal bar between the parties. The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 then adds a **registration duty**: every marriage solemnised under Muslim law shall be registered with the **Nikah Registrar** licensed for the ward or union council. Failure to report for registration is a punishable offence — simple imprisonment and fine, with Punjab having enhanced the penalties by amendment — but the Ordinance punishes the people, not the marriage. Courts have held consistently that non-registration does not, by itself, invalidate the nikah.

The hierarchy of consequences is therefore practical: an unregistered marriage survives religiously and legally, but every downstream document — NADRA's marriage registration certificate (MRC), spouse visas, inheritance mutations, the wife's claims to dower and maintenance, the children's parentage record — becomes harder, slower, and contestable.

## Proving a marriage when the record is missing

When a spouse dies, or denies the marriage, or an authority demands proof, the available evidence typically includes:

- The **couple's copy of the nikahnama**, even unregistered — signed by the parties, witnesses and nikah khwan.
- **Testimony** of the nikah khwan and the attesting witnesses.
- **The nikah khwan's own register**, which licensed registrars must maintain.
- **Conduct evidence**: long cohabitation as husband and wife, the children's birth records naming both parents, the B-form showing the family unit, photographs of the ceremony.

Muslim law and the law of evidence assist here: prolonged cohabitation with recognition as spouses raises a strong presumption of marriage. Where denial is active — typically after a death, with inheritance at stake — the forum is the **family court**, through a declaratory suit regarding the marriage's existence (the family court schedule covers jactitation of marriage and connected matrimonial declarations). The decree then anchors every later registration.

## Late registration, step by step

1. **Locate the original record.** Start at the Union Council where the nikah took place: registrars deposit nikahnama volumes with the council, and many "unregistered" marriages turn out to be registered but never followed by the computerised certificate. A certified copy ends the problem immediately.
2. **If genuinely unregistered**, apply to the Union Council concerned for registration of the marriage, with the nikahnama copy, CNICs, witnesses and (where asked) affidavits. Councils handle straightforward, uncontested cases administratively; the secretary records the entry and the registration proceeds, though practice varies between councils and the applicable fees change — check locally.
3. **If the council refuses or the matter is contested**, obtain a declaratory decree from the family court first, then present it for registration; councils act on decrees without argument.
4. **Complete the NADRA layer.** With the Union Council record, obtain the computerised **MRC** — the document embassies and foreign authorities actually want — and update marital status on both CNICs.

For overseas Pakistanis the sequence is identical but runs through an attorney at home: a properly attested special power of attorney lets a relative pursue the council and court steps.

## Correcting errors in the nikahnama record

Mistakes — a misspelled name, a wrong CNIC digit, an incorrect date, dower recorded wrongly — split into two classes. **Clerical errors**, where the original intention is obvious from the record itself, are corrected on application to the Union Council, supported by CNICs and the original entries. **Substantive disputes** — the dower amount, whether column 18 was filled, an alleged forged entry — cannot be "corrected" administratively; they need a declaratory suit, because changing them alters rights. Never overwrite or interpolate on a nikahnama copy yourself: an altered document is worse than a wrong one, and converts a correction problem into a forgery allegation.

## Protecting yourself going forward

At any new nikah in the family, insist on three things: the nikah khwan's licence for that union council, the registration number on the spot, and a certified copy from the council within days — not the loose carbon copy that sits in a cupboard for a decade. Ten minutes at registration saves the years this article describes.

Whether the task is proving an old village nikah, registering it late, or correcting a wrong entry, the route depends entirely on what record survives. A brief consultation with whatever papers exist — even a single carbon copy — usually settles the sequence in one sitting.
