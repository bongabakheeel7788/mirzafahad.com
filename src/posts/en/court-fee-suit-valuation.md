No one loses sleep over court fee until an objection surfaces two years into a suit — and suddenly the case is about stamps, not rights. Court fee and valuation are the plumbing of civil litigation: invisible when done right, ruinous when done wrong. Twenty minutes of care at filing avoids years of collateral litigation.

## Two different valuations

Every civil suit carries two valuations that beginners conflate. **Valuation for court fee**, governed by the **Court Fees Act 1870**, determines what you pay the state to sue. **Valuation for jurisdiction**, governed by the **Suits Valuation Act 1887**, determines which court hears the case and where appeals go. Often the two coincide; in several suit types the law lets them differ. Your plaint must state both, and an artificially low jurisdictional value — chosen to stay before a preferred court — is a standing invitation to a jurisdictional objection.

## Ad valorem versus fixed fee

The Court Fees Act splits reliefs into two baskets:

- **Ad valorem fee (Schedule I):** a sliding fee proportionate to the value claimed — suits for recovery of money, for possession of property based on title, for pre-emption, and the like. Section 7 of the Act prescribes how value is computed for each class: for money suits, the amount claimed; for certain property suits, formulas tied to revenue records or market value.
- **Fixed fee (Schedule II):** a flat amount regardless of stakes — typically suits for **declaration without consequential relief**, injunctions, and similar reliefs.

The battleground is the middle: a declaration **with consequential relief** (declare the sale deed void *and* restore my possession) attracts ad valorem fee on the consequential relief. Plaintiffs routinely draft prayers to look declaratory and pay fixed fee; defendants routinely object that the real relief is possession or recovery. Courts look at the **substance of the plaint**, not the label. The actual fee amounts and slabs are revised by the Punjab government from time to time — current figures must be checked at filing, not assumed from an old plaint.

## What deficiency costs

Underpaid court fee is not an instant death sentence — but it is a running wound:

1. The court, on noticing deficiency (often on the office objection or the defendant's application), **grants time** to make it good.
2. Failure within the time allowed means **rejection of the plaint under Order VII Rule 11 CPC** — and while a rejected plaint can sometimes be refiled, limitation may have expired in the meantime.
3. The objection can be raised **at any stage** — trial, appeal, even execution in rare forms — and an appellate court can require deficiency to be made good before hearing you.
4. Deliberate undervaluation also poisons credibility: a judge who sees valuation gamesmanship reads the rest of the plaint less charitably.

The converse trap exists too: **overvaluation** inflates fee unnecessarily and can land the suit in the wrong court, with the decree later attacked for jurisdiction.

## Where the fee burden is light by design

The legislature deliberately keeps certain doors cheap. **Family Court** suits — maintenance, dower, dowry articles, dissolution — carry a nominal fixed fee under the **Family Courts Act 1964**, so a wife's claim is never priced out of court. **Rent proceedings** before Special Tribunals under the Punjab Rented Premises Act run on nominal fees likewise. Small claims and specific summary procedures have their own light regimes. In these jurisdictions valuation fights largely disappear — one reason family and rent matters move faster than title suits.

## Practical rules at filing

- **Value the real relief.** Ask what you actually want executed at the end; that is usually what the fee law taxes.
- **State both valuations expressly** in the plaint, with the section 7 basis for the computation.
- **Check current Punjab fee rates** — amounts change; the mechanism does not.
- **Keep proof of payment** (e-stamp or court fee stamps) firmly attached to the record.
- If sued, **scrutinise the plaintiff's valuation early** — a sound objection at the first hearing is worth three at arguments.

Valuation questions are answered in minutes with the right documents in hand — the title papers, the revenue entries, the amount genuinely in dispute. A brief consultation before filing fixes the valuation, the forum and the fee in one pass, which is exactly where this battle should be won.
