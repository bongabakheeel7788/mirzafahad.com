The housing society file is modern Punjab's most ambiguous asset: part contract, part hope, part hostage. Societies levy "development charges" on plots paid off a decade ago, invent transfer fees at the moment a sale is finally arranged, and meet objections with the phrase *society ke rules hain*. This article is about where those rules actually stand in law — and where they collapse.

## What a society legally is

A private housing scheme is a creature of documents, not sovereignty: the cooperative or company instruments that constitute it, its **approved layout and scheme sanctioned by the development authority** (LDA, FDA, TMA or the relevant body), its bylaws, and — decisive for each member — the **allotment letter, payment schedule and terms you signed**. Everything the society may demand from you must trace to one of these. A charge that traces to none of them is not a rule; it is an invoice hoping to be paid.

## The classic overcharges, and their answers

**Post-payment "development charges".** The society sold at a price covering development, then bills again for what it already sold. The answer lives in your allotment terms: where they fix the price and describe included development, later levies beyond genuine, provided-for cost escalations are contractual breach dressed as policy — resisted and recovered through the regulator and courts by members holding their paperwork.

**Transfer and NOC fees.** Reasonable, bylaws-based transfer charges are lawful; the abuse is their inflation at the moment of maximum leverage — your buyer waiting, your deal expiring. Demand the fee schedule's source in writing before paying under protest, and know that "pay first, dispute never" is exactly the calculation the society is making about you.

**Possession that never comes.** Full payment against a plot that stays paper is the gravest version: the remedies run from formal demand, to complaint before the cooperative department or development authority (whose scheme approvals give them leverage societies respect), to litigation for possession or refund **with compensation** — and, where the scheme was sold beyond its approvals, into the territory our property-fraud article covers.

**Utilities and dues as weapons.** Blocking utility connections, or withholding transfer, to force disputed dues is coercion the forums recognise as such; pay the undisputed, contest the rest formally, and document the coercion — it strengthens every subsequent claim.

## The forums

Cooperative societies answer to the **cooperative department** and its dispute machinery; company-form schemes to their regulators; all schemes to the **development authority** that sanctioned them, whose complaint cells act on layout and delivery violations. The **consumer forum** has been used against societies as service providers; the civil court remains the general route for contract enforcement, and members acting collectively — as in every collective-action arena in this series — achieve what individual complainants rarely do.

## The file that wins

Allotment letter, every receipt, the brochure and map you bought against, the fee schedules, and every demand the society has issued. Members lose these disputes by paperwork attrition, not law: societies bet, usually correctly, that a file assembled over fifteen years no longer exists. Keep yours — scanned, backed up — and the phrase *society ke rules hain* becomes what it always was: an invitation to ask *kaunsa rule, kis dastavez mein?*
