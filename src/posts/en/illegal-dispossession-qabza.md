"Qabza" — the forcible or fraudulent occupation of another's property — is common enough in Punjab to have produced its own vocabulary and its own statute. Victims usually lose time not because remedies are lacking but because they pick the wrong one. Here is the toolkit, matched to situations.

## The Illegal Dispossession Act, 2005

This statute was written for the qabza problem. It criminalises dispossessing an owner or lawful occupier of immovable property, and — its decisive feature — empowers the court to **restore possession to the victim during or at the conclusion of the trial**, not merely punish the occupier. Complaints go directly to the Court of Session, which can take cognizance, order police investigation, and pass interim orders for restoration.

Its scope, as developed by the courts, reaches property grabbers broadly — earlier debate about confining it to organised "qabza groups" resolved in favour of protecting victims against dispossessors generally. What the complainant must show is straightforward: lawful possession or ownership, and dispossession without lawful authority.

**Use it when:** you were in possession — as owner, tenant, or lawful occupier — and someone took or is taking the property by force, fraud or stealth. Its speed and the restoration power make it the primary weapon for recent dispossession.

## The civil suits

**Suit for possession on title:** the full ownership battle — appropriate where the dispute is really about who owns, not just who occupied. Slower, but conclusive.

**The possessory suit (section 9, Specific Relief Act):** elegantly narrow — a person dispossessed *without consent and without due course of law* can recover possession by proving only **prior possession and dispossession within six months**. Title is irrelevant; even an owner who ejects an occupier by force can be put back out by this suit. Its lesson runs both ways: victims have a fast civil track, and owners must never use self-help against occupiers — the law forces everyone through legal process.

**Injunctions:** where dispossession is threatened but not complete, a suit with a temporary injunction preserving possession is the cheapest protection. Move before the bulldozer, not after.

## The magistrate's emergency jurisdiction

Section 145 Cr.P.C. lets the Magistrate intervene where a land dispute threatens a **breach of the peace** — determining who was in possession and forbidding disturbance until civil rights are settled, with the power to attach the property in urgent cases. It is a peace-keeping order, not a title decision, but it stabilises explosive situations quickly.

## The criminal provisions

Ordinary criminal law also applies: criminal trespass, house-trespass, mischief, and where force or arms were involved, the corresponding PPC offences support an FIR. Anti-encroachment and specialised provincial task-force mechanisms operate against occupation of state land. FIRs create pressure and document events, but on their own they do not restore possession — pair them with the Act or the civil track.

## Choosing correctly — three scenarios

*You returned from abroad to find relatives' locks on your house:* Illegal Dispossession Act complaint (restoration), with a civil declaration if they assert ownership documents.

*A neighbouring influential has started construction over your boundary:* civil suit with urgent injunction, demarcation through revenue staff, and section 145 if confrontation looms.

*Your tenant stopped paying and claims the property is his:* this is not qabza but a tenancy/title dispute — eviction before the Rent Tribunal, and a title suit if he produces documents; the Act is not a substitute for eviction proceedings.

## The universal rules

Document possession continuously — utility bills, photographs, tax receipts, tenancy record: the party who can *prove* possession wins the early rounds. Report early: every remedy above works best within days or months, and the six-month possessory suit expires exactly when victims finish "trying to settle within the family". And never retake property by force, however righteous the claim — the law restores the patient and punishes the self-helper, on both sides of every qabza.
