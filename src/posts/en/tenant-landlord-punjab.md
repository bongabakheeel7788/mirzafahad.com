Most tenancy disputes in Punjab begin the same way: there is no written agreement, the rent was being paid in cash, and one side's circumstances changed. The Punjab Rented Premises Act, 2009 governs these relationships, and it rewards the party who followed its forms. Whether you are the landlord of one shop or the tenant of a house, here is what the law actually provides.

## The written agreement is not optional

The Act requires a tenancy to be in writing and registered with the Rent Registrar. An unwritten tenancy is not void — the relationship still exists — but the party relying on oral terms fights uphill. For landlords, an unregistered oral tenancy makes eviction slower; for tenants, it makes every agreed term (rent amount, duration, what the advance was for) deniable. The agreement should state rent, due date, duration, annual increase, the amount and nature of any advance or security deposit, and who pays utilities and repairs.

## Rent and increases

Rent is what the parties agree. The Act provides for the agreed annual increase; where the agreement is silent, a statutory basis for periodic increase applies. A landlord cannot simply announce a doubled rent mid-term — and a tenant cannot freeze rent forever by refusing to sign anything. If the landlord refuses to accept rent (a classic pre-eviction tactic), the tenant's protection is to deposit the rent with the Rent Tribunal rather than accumulate "default" — a tenant who stops paying because "he wouldn't take it" hands the landlord the eviction ground.

## Grounds for eviction

A landlord can seek eviction on grounds including: **default** in payment of rent within the time allowed; **expiry of the tenancy period** stated in the written agreement; **personal bona fide need** of the landlord for the premises; the building requiring **reconstruction** which cannot be carried out without vacation; the tenant **subletting** without consent; and misuse of or material damage to the premises. Each ground has its own proof requirements — "personal need", for instance, must be genuine, and tenants can and do contest it.

## The eviction process — faster than people think

Eviction petitions go to the **Rent Tribunal** (a designated court), not the ordinary civil court, and the Act's procedure is deliberately quick. A crucial feature: where the tenancy or the default is effectively undeniable, the Tribunal can require the tenant to deposit the rent as a condition of contesting — and failure to deposit leads to a summary eviction order. Well-prepared eviction cases in Punjab conclude in months, not the years people assume from older laws.

Equally, tenants have real defences: payment receipts, bank transfer records, proof the "personal need" is a pretext, or that the alleged default arose from the landlord refusing rent. The Tribunal's orders are appealable in limited ways, so the first hearing matters.

## Security deposits and advances

Disputes about the "advance" are constant because the word covers three different things: a security deposit (refundable on vacation, subject to dues and damage), advance rent (adjustable against the final months), and, in commercial lettings, goodwill (pagri) — which the law does not favour and which becomes nearly unrecoverable if undocumented. Write down which one the payment is. Landlords should record the condition of the premises at handover; tenants should insist the deposit amount and refund terms appear in the agreement.

## Practical rules

For landlords: register the agreement, issue receipts or take rent through the bank, never accept rent partially "in cash without record" during a dispute, and act on default promptly. For tenants: pay through the bank, keep every receipt, respond to legal notices in writing, and if the landlord refuses rent, deposit it before the Tribunal the same month. In tenancy litigation, the file of the party with the paperwork almost writes the judgment itself.
