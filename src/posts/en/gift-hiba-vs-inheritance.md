It is one of the most common questions asked of any lawyer in Punjab: *"My father transferred everything to my brother before he died — can he do that?"* The honest answer is layered: the law of gift permits an owner to give his property to whomever he chooses in his lifetime, and the law of evidence makes defeating a genuine gift very hard — but the courts examine gifts that disinherit natural heirs with searching suspicion, and a large share of them fail that examination.

## What a valid hiba requires

A gift under Muslim law needs three things, all during the donor's lifetime: a **declaration** of the gift by the donor; **acceptance** by the donee; and **delivery of possession** — actual or constructive — of the gifted property. No writing is strictly required by personal law, though transfers of immovable property interact with registration and revenue requirements, and an unregistered, unwitnessed, unpossessed "gift" is a phantom.

The third element decides most cases. A father who "gifted" a house he continued to occupy, whose utility bills stayed in his name, whose rent he kept collecting, delivered nothing. The gift's paperwork then stands against the reality of possession — and reality usually wins.

## So can a parent disinherit?

**In principle:** an owner in full possession of his faculties may gift his property in his lifetime to anyone — a child, some children, a stranger — and inheritance operates only on what he owns at death. There is no forced-heirship restraint on lifetime transfers as such.

**In practice:** when a gift conveniently strips the estate in favour of the very person who controlled the donor's affairs, the law's protective doctrines engage:

- **The burden shifts.** Where a transaction is between a person in a dominant position and one who reposed confidence in them — a caretaker son and an aged, ailing or illiterate father, a brother managing a secluded sister's affairs — the **beneficiary** must prove the transaction was made with free will, full understanding and independent advice.
- **Deathbed gifts (marz-ul-maut).** A gift made during the illness from which the donor dies is treated like a bequest: effective only to the extent of one-third of the estate, and not in favour of an heir without the other heirs' consent.
- **Pardanashin and illiterate donors.** Gifts by secluded or illiterate women are valid only on stringent proof that the document was read over, explained and understood — the standard instrument of family fraud being precisely the thumb-printed paper never explained.
- **Evidence of the transaction itself.** Who were the witnesses? Who paid for the stamp papers? Where was possession delivered, and how did the revenue entry occur? Gifts that surface only after death, mutations attested without the donor's presence, and marginal witnesses from the beneficiary's household all corrode credibility.

## Challenging — or defending — a gift

An heir challenging a gift files a declaratory suit: that the gift deed/mutation is the result of fraud, misrepresentation or want of the gift's essential elements, and that the property therefore falls into the inheritance. Limitation runs from **knowledge** of the fraud, not the date of the document — but delay after knowledge is fatal, so act when you learn.

Defending a genuine gift is about the same evidence in reverse: contemporaneous registration, disinterested witnesses, actual transfer of possession (occupation, rent, bills, tax records), and the donor's demonstrated independence. Parents who truly wish to prefer one child — for instance one who cared for them for decades — serve that intention best by doing the transfer openly, formally, with independent witnesses, delivered possession, and ideally the knowledge of the other children. Secrecy is what courts read as fraud.

## The bottom line

A living owner's genuine, completed gift stands. A paper gift constructed around a dying or dependent parent to defeat sisters and brothers usually falls. Which side of that line a case sits on is a question of evidence — and evidence is gathered fastest by whoever moves first.
