Industrial Faisalabad and its surrounding tehsils run on labour that is largely undocumented, and when a worker loses fingers to a power loom or a life to a factory roof, the family is routinely told the employer's mercy is the only compensation on offer. The law says otherwise, in three overlapping systems that even many educated families have never heard named.

## No-fault employer liability

The workmen's compensation framework — the long-standing statutory scheme as applied in Punjab — makes the **employer liable for personal injury arising out of and in the course of employment**, without the worker proving negligence: the machine's guard was missing or the worker slipped unremarkably, the liability follows the employment. Compensation follows statutory computation tied to wages and the injury's gravity — death, permanent total disablement, permanent partial disablement per the schedules of lost capacity, and temporary disablement through the recovery period. Claims go before the **Commissioner for Workmen's Compensation** (the designated authority in the district labour apparatus), on limitation clocks that make early filing important, and the forum is built to be accessible to workmen and dependents directly.

Two features matter practically. The framework's wage records presumption problem cuts *against* the informal employer: employment can be proved by any evidence — co-workers, gate registers, wage witnesses, the accident's own circumstances — and the employer who kept no records does not thereby escape. And **death cases belong to the dependents**: widows, children and dependent parents claim in their own right, with the amount deposited and distributed under the Commissioner's supervision precisely so it cannot be quietly settled with one relative.

## The social security stream

For establishments within **PESSI** (Punjab Employees Social Security Institution) coverage, a registered — or registrable — worker's injury engages a second system: free medical treatment at social security facilities, **injury benefit** during incapacity, and for permanent disablement or death, **pensions** to the worker or survivors. Where the employer should have registered the worker and did not, the failure is the employer's violation, pursued as such — a lever that transforms negotiations. The EOBI old-age framework runs alongside with its own survivors' pensions for covered employment.

## Beyond the schedules: employer fault

Where injury flows from genuine employer fault — removed guards, ordered risk-taking, absent safety equipment in work that legally required it — the civil action for damages, and in grave cases the criminal law on negligence, remain available beyond the no-fault schedules; factories legislation imposes the safety duties whose breach grounds such claims. These routes are slower and evidence-hungry, but in catastrophic cases they address what schedules cannot.

## What workers and families should actually do

From the accident's first day: medical treatment with the injury's cause **recorded in the medical papers**; names and numbers of co-worker witnesses; photographs of the machine or site where possible; and every document connecting worker to workplace — cards, chits, salary envelopes, the supervisor's WhatsApp messages. Refuse quiet settlements pressed in the first grieving week; any settlement worth signing can be signed after the family knows the statutory arithmetic. Then file — before the Commissioner for compensation, with PESSI where coverage exists — and treat the labour department's inspection machinery as an ally the employer answers to.

The system is imperfect and its offices move at their own pace. But its central promise is real and enforced: in Pakistani law, an injured worker's compensation is a right computed by statute — not a favour measured by the seth's mood.
