Almost every overseas Pakistani eventually needs someone at home to act for them — to sell or manage property, pursue a case, collect rent, or complete an inheritance. The instrument for that is a power of attorney (mukhtar-nama), and it is also the single most misused document in Pakistani property fraud. This guide covers both halves: making one that works, and limiting it so it cannot be turned against you.

## The two routes

**Embassy attestation (the classic route).** The power of attorney is drafted, the principal signs it before the Pakistani embassy or consulate in their country of residence with two witnesses, and the mission attests it. Back in Pakistan, that attested document must then be stamped (within the statutory period after receipt in Pakistan) and, where it deals with immovable property, registered with the Sub-Registrar or at least authenticated for use before the revenue authorities.

**NADRA's online route.** NADRA now offers overseas Pakistanis a digital power-of-attorney service through Pak-Identity, with video verification of the principal and biometric linkage of the parties — considerably faster than the embassy queue and increasingly accepted. Check that the authority you will use it before (a particular housing society, a bank) accepts the NADRA-format document; acceptance has grown but is not yet universal.

## What makes a POA valid — and what defeats it

A POA is construed strictly. The attorney can do only what the document plainly authorises, so vague drafting produces disputes. A **general** power of attorney conveys broad management authority; a **special** power of attorney authorises named acts. For anything involving sale of property, the document must say so expressly — and courts read powers of sale narrowly.

Common defects that surface years later: attestation by a notary of the foreign country only, without Pakistani mission attestation; failure to stamp in Pakistan; use of a photocopy where the original is required; and the principal's CNIC/NICOP having expired at execution.

## The safeguards that matter

The typical fraud is not a forged POA — it is a genuine one used beyond its purpose. Protect yourself:

- **Never grant a general power of sale "to be safe".** Grant exactly what the task needs: "to manage and rent out House No. X" is not "to sell".
- **Name the property specifically.** An attorney with power over "all my properties" can surprise you.
- **Exclude self-dealing expressly**: state that the attorney may not sell or transfer to himself, his spouse or children.
- **Require sale proceeds into your named bank account**, stated in the document.
- **Prefer a special POA with an expiry date** for one-off transactions.
- **Register the POA** — a registered document is on the public record, and so is its revocation.

## Revocation

A POA can be revoked at any time (unless coupled with interest). Revoke in writing, register the revocation deed where the POA was registered, and give written notice to the attorney and to any authority where the POA might be used — the housing society, the tehsil office, the bank. An unrevoked POA in the hands of an estranged relative is a standing risk; an unnoticed revocation may not bind third parties who dealt in good faith.

## A note on death

A power of attorney **dies with the principal**. Transactions signed by the attorney after the principal's death are void, and using a dead person's POA is fraud. On a principal's death, the property passes to the heirs, and the attorney must stop — heirs discovering post-death transfers should act immediately, because time strengthens the paper trail of the wrongdoer.

For overseas Pakistanis, the practical bottom line: a well-drafted, narrow, registered POA plus a trustworthy local lawyer who reports to you is cheaper than one year of litigation from abroad.
