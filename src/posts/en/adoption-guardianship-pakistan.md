Thousands of Pakistani couples raise children born to others — a brother's son given in childhood, an orphaned niece, a foundling from an Edhi cradle — and almost all of them discover, usually at school-admission or passport time, that "we adopted him" has no direct legal meaning in Pakistan. The law offers something different and, once understood, workable: guardianship, NADRA registration, and deliberate estate planning. Families that complete all three steps give the child real security; families that stop at love alone leave the child exposed.

## Why there is no adoption statute for Muslims

Islamic law recognises and rewards raising another's child — *kafalah* — but preserves the child's true parentage: the child keeps the biological father's name and lineage, and the prohibitions and inheritance rules follow blood, not upbringing. Pakistani law tracks this position for Muslims; there is no procedure that extinguishes the birth parents' legal relationship and substitutes the adoptive parents, as a Western adoption decree does. For non-Muslim citizens, courts have in some cases accommodated adoption under personal or foreign law, but the mainstream route for everyone remains guardianship.

## Step one: the guardianship certificate

The **Guardians and Wards Act 1890** lets any person claim appointment as guardian of a minor's person (and property, if any) from the **Guardian Judge** — in practice the family court. The procedure:

1. **Petition** before the Guardian Judge of the place where the minor ordinarily resides, stating the child's parentage (or that it is unknown, for a foundling), how the child came into your care, and why appointment serves the child's welfare.
2. **Notice** to the biological parents or surviving relatives, who may consent — a written, verified consent from the natural parents is the backbone of a smooth case — or contest.
3. **Welfare inquiry.** The paramount consideration is the **welfare of the minor**: the court weighs the petitioners' means, home, character and relationship to the child. For abandoned infants taken from welfare organisations, the organisation's record substitutes for parental consent.
4. **Certificate.** The court issues a guardianship certificate, often with conditions — commonly a bar on removing the minor from Pakistan without the court's permission, which matters enormously for families planning to settle abroad; a specific permission must be sought for that.

The certificate does not make you the parent; it makes you the lawful custodian, answerable to the court, until the child attains majority.

## Step two: NADRA registration

With the guardianship certificate, register the child with NADRA: the birth registration through the union council if not already done, the **child registration certificate (B-form)** linking the child to your family record, and in due course the CNIC. NADRA's handling of the parentage columns in guardianship cases has evolved through policy — including arrangements for children of unknown parentage — and documentary requirements shift, so confirm the current checklist at the registration office. What stays constant: without the court certificate, NADRA registration in your family record is where informal adoptions hit the wall, and families are tempted into the serious mistake of recording a false parentage, which is an offence and a time-bomb for the child's documents.

## Step three: the inheritance plan

This is the step most families miss. An adopted child is **not a legal heir** of the guardians under Muslim personal law: on your death, your estate devolves on your blood heirs, and the child you raised takes nothing by operation of law. Two instruments fix this:

- **Gift (hiba) during your lifetime** — the cleanest tool. Property transferred by a completed gift, with possession delivered and the mutation or registered deed done, belongs to the child outright and never enters the estate.
- **Will (wasiyyat)** — valid in favour of a non-heir up to **one-third of the net estate** without the heirs' consent; anything beyond needs their consent after your death.

Combine them: gift the house, will the savings, and record both properly. Conversely, the child retains full inheritance rights in the **biological** parents' estates — a point worth preserving in family records rather than obscuring.

## Common failure patterns

- No court certificate, only an informal family arrangement — collapses at NADRA, school board, or visa stage.
- False parentage entries — criminal exposure and documents that unravel decades later.
- No gift or will — the child disinherited by default, at the mercy of your heirs.
- Taking the child abroad without the court's removal permission — breach of the certificate's terms.

Each situation — a relative's child, a foundling, a widowed daughter-in-law's children — has its own correct sequence of petition, consent and paperwork. A brief consultation with whatever documents exist, even just the child's birth record, usually settles the whole route in one sitting.
