Haq mehr is the one financial right in a Muslim marriage that is the wife's alone — not her father's, not maintenance, not a gift that can be taken back. Yet in practice it is routinely written into the nikahnama and then forgotten, treated as a formality rather than the enforceable debt it is. Pakistani law treats unpaid mehr very seriously, and the family court is built to recover it.

## The two kinds, and where they are written

Mehr comes in two legal forms. **Prompt mehr (mehr muajjal)** is payable immediately — the wife may demand it the day after the nikah, and refusal gives her a cause of action at once. **Deferred mehr (mehr muwajjal)** is postponed until the marriage dissolves, whether by talaq, by khula, or by the husband's death.

The nikahnama records the split in **columns 13 to 16**: the total amount, how much is prompt, how much deferred, and whether any part was paid at the time of marriage in cash or property. Read your own nikahnama; many families discover years later that the nikah registrar wrote something nobody discussed.

Where the nikahnama is silent on the mode of payment, **section 10 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961** supplies the answer: the entire amount is presumed payable **on demand**. That presumption favours the wife, and courts apply it routinely.

## Mehr in property, gold or land

Mehr need not be cash. Nikahnamas in Punjab commonly fix mehr as gold ornaments by weight, a house, or a share in agricultural land. Two practical points:

- **Gold by weight is the safest drafting.** A rupee figure fixed in 1995 is worthless today; five tolas of gold holds its value. Courts decree gold mehr at the weight written, payable in kind or at the market value on the date of the decree.
- **Land written as mehr should be mutated.** If the nikahnama gives the wife a specific plot, have the intiqal (mutation) entered in the revenue record promptly. An unmutated promise is still enforceable, but enforcement against heirs decades later is a harder suit than it needed to be.

## Recovering mehr through the family court

A suit for recovery of dower sits in the **Family Court under the Family Courts Act 1964** — dower is item two of the Schedule. The practical features:

1. **It can be joined with other claims.** A wife suing for maintenance or dissolution routinely adds the mehr claim in the same consolidated suit; one case, one court.
2. **Prompt mehr: sue after demand and refusal.** Limitation runs three years from the demand being refused — or, if no demand was ever made during the marriage, from dissolution. Do not let a refused demand sit for years.
3. **Deferred mehr: sue after dissolution.** The cause of action arises on divorce or death, with limitation running three years from that date.
4. **Khula does not automatically forfeit mehr.** In khula the wife typically returns the benefit she received — commonly the prompt mehr actually paid — but deferred mehr that was never paid is dealt with by the court on the facts. Do not assume khula means walking away from everything; have the decree's terms read carefully.

The family court's procedure is simpler and faster than a civil suit — no court fee battles on the same scale, relaxed evidence rules, and a decree executable through the same court.

## When the husband dies

Unpaid mehr becomes a **debt against the deceased husband's estate**, and debts are paid before inheritance shares are distributed. The widow therefore claims twice over: first the mehr as a creditor, then her inheritance share (one-eighth with children, one-fourth without) from what remains.

Classical law adds a powerful tool the courts still recognise: the **widow's right of retention**. A widow lawfully in possession of her husband's property may retain that possession until her dower is paid — she does not become owner, but the heirs cannot eject her while the debt stands. In village disputes over a deceased man's house, this right is often the widow's strongest card and is regularly overlooked.

## Common traps

- **"Mehr was forgiven."** Heirs frequently claim the wife forgave the mehr on the deathbed. Courts scrutinise such waivers hard; a forgiveness extracted in grief or under family pressure, without free consent, does not stand.
- **Token entries.** A nikahnama showing a nominal figure because "mehr sharai" was intended still binds at the written figure unless rectified — fix the document, not the memory.
- **Paying the father.** Mehr paid to the wife's father or brother without her authority is not payment to her.

Whether you are drafting a nikahnama, demanding unpaid mehr, or defending an estate, the entire strategy usually turns on the nikahnama's four columns and the revenue record. A brief consultation with those documents in hand settles the right course quickly.
