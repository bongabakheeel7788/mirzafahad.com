Divorce by the husband — talaq — is the area of family law where the gap between religious practice and legal procedure causes the most damage. A man who pronounces talaq and considers the matter closed, without following the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, creates years of uncertainty for both spouses. The procedure is short; skipping it is expensive.

## The required procedure

Under section 7 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961, a man who pronounces talaq **must give written notice to the chairman of the Union Council** (of the wife's residence area) and supply a copy to the wife. From the date the notice reaches the chairman:

1. The chairman constitutes an **Arbitration Council** for reconciliation between the parties.
2. A period of **ninety days** runs. The talaq is *not* effective when pronounced — it becomes effective on the expiry of ninety days from the notice (or, if the wife is pregnant, upon the later of the ninety days or the end of the pregnancy), unless revoked earlier.
3. If reconciliation fails, the Union Council issues the **certificate of effectiveness of talaq** after the period expires.

That certificate is the document the world relies on: NADRA updates records on it, remarriage is proven safe by it, and dower and maintenance consequences date from it.

## Why the notice matters so much

**For the wife:** without notice and the certificate, her status is dangerously ambiguous. She may believe herself divorced and remarry — and historically, missing notices exposed women to bigamy allegations. The notice procedure exists substantially for her protection.

**For the husband:** an unnotified talaq leaves the marriage legally alive. Maintenance obligations continue. Inheritance rights continue — if he dies, the "divorced" wife may inherit as a widow; if she dies, he inherits. And a pronouncement he claims happened years ago, produced later in litigation, is viewed with suspicion precisely because the statutory route was available and avoided.

Failure to give notice is also, in itself, a punishable offence under the Ordinance.

## Revocation and the halala misconception

Within the ninety days, the talaq can be revoked — expressly or by resuming the marital relationship — and the notice withdrawn. This is the law's cooling-off design working as intended.

A related point on which misinformation abounds: under the Ordinance, remarriage between the same couple after an effective talaq is lawful **without an intervening marriage**, unless the talaq has been effective for the third time. So-called "halala" arrangements marketed after a first or second talaq are religiously controversial, legally unnecessary and frequently exploitative.

## During and after the ninety days

The wife is entitled to maintenance during the iddat period. The **deferred dower (mehr mu'wajjal) falls due on divorce** — a man planning talaq should plan for this debt, because the Family Court will decree it swiftly. Children's maintenance continues unaffected; custody follows the welfare principles that apply generally.

## The mistakes, summarised

Pronouncing talaq in anger and telling no one; giving no Union Council notice; giving notice to the wrong Union Council (it belongs where the wife resides); assuming three pronouncements in one sitting concluded everything instantly; and treating the certificate as optional paperwork. Each of these keeps the marriage — and its financial obligations — legally breathing.

Done properly, the procedure is a matter of one written notice, attendance before the Arbitration Council, and ninety days of patience. Done improperly, it becomes the first exhibit in the other side's case.
