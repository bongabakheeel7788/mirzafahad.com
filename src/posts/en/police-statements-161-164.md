In every FIR-driven case, two kinds of recorded words float around the file: what people told the **police** during investigation, and what someone said before a **magistrate**. Families treat both as fate — "he has given a statement against us" — but the law weighs them very differently, and knowing the difference changes how you handle an investigation from day one.

## Section 161: the police statement

During investigation, the investigating officer examines anyone acquainted with the facts and records their versions under **section 161 CrPC**. Three features define these statements:

- **They are not signed.** Section 162 CrPC expressly prohibits the witness from signing, precisely because the legislature distrusted investigation-stage records — statements can be shaped, padded or invented by the recording officer.
- **They are not substantive evidence.** A 161 statement cannot itself prove any fact. The witness must enter the witness box at trial; only that testimony, tested by cross-examination, is evidence.
- **Their main use is contradiction.** The defence can confront a prosecution witness with his earlier 161 statement to show improvement, omission or contradiction — the daily bread of cross-examination in our courts. A witness who told the police one story and the court a better one loses credit, and material improvements are routinely discounted by judges.

So a hostile 161 statement against you is a problem to manage, not a verdict. Conversely, as an accused you should know that **a confession made to a police officer is inadmissible** — Articles 38 and 39 of the **Qanun-e-Shahadat Order 1984** shut that door, whatever pressure produced it. The narrow exception is Article 40: information from an accused in custody is admissible only to the limited extent it **distinctly leads to discovery** of a fact — a weapon recovered from a pointed-out place — and even then only that connecting portion.

## Section 164: the magistrate's record

**Section 164 CrPC** lets a magistrate record statements and confessions during investigation. The setting is deliberately different: a judicial officer, an open record, and for confessions the safeguards of **section 364 CrPC** and settled practice — the magistrate must warn the accused that he is not bound to confess, that the confession can be used against him, ensure police are out of the room and out of the equation, allow time for reflection, and certify voluntariness.

Two distinct uses follow:

1. **Witness statements under 164** — often recorded where a witness may be won over or silenced later. These still are **not substantive evidence**: the witness must testify at trial. The 164 statement then serves to corroborate consistent testimony or contradict a witness who resiles. Its real function is to make flipping expensive.
2. **Confessions under 164** — the only confession route the law trusts. A properly recorded judicial confession is substantive evidence against its maker, and can be considered against co-accused only as a weak, corroborative circumstance, never the foundation of their conviction.

## Retraction — the standard script

Most judicial confessions in Pakistan are retracted at trial, usually with allegations of police torture or deception before production. Retraction does not erase the confession; the court asks two questions: was it **voluntary** (scrutinising custody timeline, injuries, time for reflection, the magistrate's certificate) and is it **true** (checking fit with the proven facts)? As a settled rule of prudence, courts seek **independent corroboration** before convicting on a retracted confession alone. Defects in recording — police presence, no warning, immediate return to police custody — can destroy its value entirely.

## Practical guidance

- **As a witness:** say what you know, accurately and once. Exaggerations in a 161 statement become contradiction fodder that can collapse the very case you support.
- **As an accused:** you cannot be forced to confess; anything told to police is inadmissible as confession, and you are entitled to counsel. If produced before a magistrate for a 164 confession, understand you may decline — the warning is not a formality.
- **As a complainant:** get crucial, vulnerable witnesses recorded under 164 early; it anchors the case against later pressure.
- **Preserve the record:** certified copies of 161 and 164 statements obtained at the proper stage drive the cross-examination that wins trials.

How much a particular statement helps or hurts depends on its exact words against the rest of the file. A brief consultation with the FIR, the statements and the challan usually settles what the recorded words are truly worth — and what to do about them.
