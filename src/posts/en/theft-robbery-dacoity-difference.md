Families often arrive at a lawyer's office saying "a theft case has been registered" when the FIR actually cites section 392 or 395 PPC — and the difference is not academic. It decides the maximum sentence, the realistic chance of bail, and whether the matter can ever be quietly compromised. The three offences sit on a ladder, and each rung is defined by one added ingredient.

## Theft: section 378, punished under 379

Theft is the base offence: **dishonestly taking movable property out of someone's possession without consent**. Five points define it — dishonest intention, movable property, taking it out of another's possession, absence of consent, and some movement of the property. Punishment under section 379 PPC runs up to three years' imprisonment, fine, or both.

Two practical notes. First, property taken from a house or building attracts the aggravated form (section 380) with a higher ceiling. Second, theft is about **possession**, not ownership — taking back your own motorcycle from a mechanic who lawfully holds it for unpaid repair charges can still be theft.

## Robbery: section 390, punished under 392

Robbery is theft plus **violence or the fear of instant violence**. Under section 390 PPC, theft becomes robbery when, in order to commit the theft or carry away the property, the offender voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt or wrongful restraint — or the instant fear of them. The word *instant* is the hinge: a threat of future harm does not make a robbery.

Punishment under section 392 is rigorous imprisonment from three to ten years plus fine; robbery committed on a highway between sunset and sunrise carries a ceiling of fourteen years. Street snatching of mobile phones and motorcycles at gunpoint — the commonest urban crime in Punjab — is legally robbery, and FIRs usually add section 411 (receiving stolen property) against anyone found with the goods later.

## Dacoity: section 391, punished under 395

Dacoity is robbery by **five or more persons acting conjointly**. The number includes those present and aiding, not only those who laid hands on the victim. Punishment under section 395 PPC is imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment of four to ten years, plus fine. Aggravated forms climb from there: dacoity with murder (section 396) exposes every member of the gang to the death penalty, and preparation or assembly for dacoity (sections 399 and 402) are offences even before anything is taken.

A parallel track exists on paper: the Offences Against Property (Enforcement of Hudood) Ordinance 1979 defines *haraabah*, but its strict proof requirements mean virtually all cases proceed under the PPC as ta'zir.

## Bail prospects, rung by rung

- **Theft (379)**: with a three-year maximum, the offence sits outside the prohibitory clause of section 497 CrPC, so bail is the rule and refusal the exception.
- **Robbery (392)**: the ten-to-fourteen-year ceiling brings it within the prohibitory clause. Bail turns on the quality of identification (was there a proper identification parade before a magistrate?), the recovery, and delay in the FIR. Weak identification evidence is the most common door to bail.
- **Dacoity (395)**: the hardest rung. Courts examine whether the accused is specifically named with a role or swept in among "unknown persons" later implicated, whether recovery was witnessed independently, and whether the parade was conducted promptly and fairly.

In all three, juveniles and women benefit from the softer bail standards discussed elsewhere in this series.

## Compromise and recovery of your property

Theft and its aggravated forms are compoundable with the court's permission in certain configurations, and in practice many theft FIRs end in compromise; robbery and dacoity are **not compoundable**, and a private "settlement" does not close the State's case, though it often softens the complainant's evidence. Victims should remember the other side of the file: stolen property traced during investigation can be returned on *superdari* (interim custody) by the magistrate — apply early with ownership proof.

Whether you are complainant or accused, the first question is always which rung of the ladder the facts truly support, because police routinely over-section FIRs. A short consultation with the FIR, recovery memos and the identification-parade record settles both the bail strategy and the trial defence.
