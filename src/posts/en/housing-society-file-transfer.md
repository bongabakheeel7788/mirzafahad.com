The "file" is Pakistan's strangest property product: people trade photocopied folders worth millions of rupees for land that may not exist yet, in phases that may never be developed, issued by societies that may not own the ground. Files also mint genuine fortunes and house real families. The difference between the two outcomes is almost always visible in the paperwork — before purchase, to anyone who knows what the letters mean.

## What a file legally is

A file is a bundle of **contractual rights against a housing society or developer**: proof you (or the person whose name is on it) subscribed for a plot of a stated size in a scheme, with payments made or scheduled. It is not land, not title, and nothing in the revenue record shows it. Your rights rise and fall with the society's own: if the society never acquired the land, or its scheme is refused approval, your file is a claim for refund against an entity that may have spent the money.

That is not a reason never to buy — it is the reason the checks below exist.

## The ladder of letters

Understand which rung a file stands on, because price and risk live on this ladder:

1. **Open file / registration:** no name registered with the society, or freely transferable by possession of the folder. Cheapest, most liquid, most dangerous — an open file can be sold several times, and whoever records first with the society wins the race.
2. **Allocation letter:** the society promises a plot of a category (size, sometimes phase) but **no plot number**. You hold a place in a queue for land that may still be unacquired or undeveloped.
3. **Allotment letter:** your file now carries a **specific plot number** in an approved plan. Risk drops substantially.
4. **Possession letter:** the society certifies development is complete enough to hand over the plot's physical possession for construction.
5. **Registered transfer / title:** depending on the scheme's structure, a registered deed or the society's transfer process recognised by the authority finally converts contract into property. Only here does the ordinary law of registry and mutation take over.

A seller quoting an allotment-letter price for an allocation-letter file is charging you for a rung the file has not climbed.

## The transfer procedure

Societies control their own registers, so a file changes hands validly only through the society's process:

- **No Demand Certificate (NDC):** obtain it before paying — it states what the society says is paid and due on this file. Arrears and surcharges follow the file, not the old owner.
- **Verification:** confirm directly with the society's office that the file is genuine, in the seller's name, and unencumbered — not pledged, blocked or cancelled.
- **Transfer appearance:** both parties (or attorneys under valid powers of attorney) appear, sign the transfer documents, pay the society's transfer fee, and complete biometric or CNIC verification where the society requires it.
- **New letter:** the buyer collects the fresh allotment or allocation letter in his own name — not a photocopy of the seller's with a receipt stapled on.

Paying the full price against a signed "file and open transfer papers" without completing the society's transfer is the classic trap: the register still shows the seller, and his creditors, heirs or second buyer deal with the society as if you did not exist.

## Scheme-level due diligence

Before any of the above, check the society itself:

- **Approval status** of the scheme and its layout plan with the development authority or municipal body having jurisdiction — approvals are phase-specific, and a society "approved" for Phase 1 may be selling files in an unapproved Phase 5.
- **Land ownership:** does the society own, through registered deeds and mutations, the land of your phase? Societies notoriously sell more files than they hold plots.
- **Litigation and notices:** schemes under regulatory notices or court injunctions trade at discounts for a reason.
- **Overseas buyers:** insist on the society's own written verification and use attorneys you actually control; file fraud disproportionately targets buyers abroad.

## Closing the file properly

A file bought at the right rung of the ladder, verified with the society, transferred through its process and carried through to possession and registered title is a normal, lawful way to acquire a plot. Each shortcut removes one of those protections. Before you pay — or if a transfer has gone wrong midway — a brief consultation with the file, the letters, the NDC and the payment record will establish exactly what you hold, what is missing, and the cleanest path to a plot that is actually yours.
