When a marriage breaks down, the question of the dowry — the furniture, appliances, clothing and above all the gold given at the wedding — becomes one of the sharpest disputes. The law here is clearer than families assume: **dowry articles and bridal gifts are the wife's property**. The Dowry and Bridal Gifts (Restriction) Act, 1976 says so expressly, and Family Courts enforce it daily.

## What counts as dowry

Dowry (jahez) is what the bride's family gives the bride; bridal gifts include what the husband's side gives her. Both vest in the wife. Gold ornaments given to the bride — from either side — are hers. The husband's family keeping "their" gold back after separation is, legally, keeping the wife's property.

What is *not* recoverable as dowry: wedding expenses themselves (the food, the hall), and gifts given to persons other than the bride.

## The proof problem — and how courts solve it

Almost no family documents a dowry. Courts know this, and the standard of proof is realistic: a **list of dowry articles**, even prepared afterwards, supported by the oath of the wife and her family witnesses, receipts where they exist, photographs and wedding video showing articles and ornaments in use, and the ordinary customs of families of the parties' standing. Courts assess claims for exaggeration — a modest household claiming fifty tolas invites skepticism — but they do not reject claims merely for want of receipts.

The practical lesson for families today: make a signed, dated list at the time of marriage, with photographs. Ten minutes of paperwork at the wedding saves a year of evidence later.

## Where and how to claim

Recovery of dowry articles is within the exclusive jurisdiction of the **Family Court**, and the claim is routinely joined with suits for maintenance and dissolution — one case, several claims. The wife files where she resides, which spares her litigating in the husband's district. The court records evidence from both sides; the husband's typical defence — "she took everything when she left" — requires its own proof and is weighed against the circumstances of the departure (a woman who left in one suit of clothes after a quarrel plainly did not carry a refrigerator).

## The decree: articles or value

Decrees are framed in the alternative: return the articles, or pay their value. Two valuation points matter enormously for gold:

- Courts increasingly value gold ornaments at the **rate prevailing at the time of recovery/decree**, not the wedding-day rate — reflecting that the wife lost the thing itself, whose value is its market price. With gold's rise, this difference can dwarf the rest of the case.
- Used household articles are valued with depreciation; gold is not depreciated.

## Execution

A dowry decree is executed like any other: attachment of the husband's property or salary, and coercive measures for non-compliance. Where articles are decreed in specie, bailiffs can recover them physically.

## Practical advice

For wives: prepare the list early, gather the wedding video and photographs, and claim in the same suit as maintenance — the combined pressure produces settlements. Do not inflate; one disproved item taints ten proved ones.

For husbands: return what is hers promptly and get a receipt through counsel. Gold sitting in a cupboard accrues liability at gold's climbing rate, and "the articles are lying at our house, she can collect them" is not a defence unless made real — formally, in writing, through the court.
