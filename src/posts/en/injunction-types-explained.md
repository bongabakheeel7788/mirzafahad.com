"Stay le lo" is the first advice every property disputant hears, and half of Punjab's civil litigation begins with an application for one. But an injunction is not a rubber stamp — it is a discretionary order with a precise legal test, and misunderstanding it costs people both their urgency and their credibility. Here is how the machinery actually works.

## The two families: temporary and permanent

A **temporary injunction** is granted during a pending suit under **Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2 CPC** — typically restraining alienation of disputed property, dispossession, construction, or encashment of an instrument — and lives only until the suit's decision or an earlier date. A **permanent (perpetual) injunction** is a final relief granted by the decree itself under the **Specific Relief Act 1877**, after full trial, permanently restraining the defendant from violating your established right. A **mandatory injunction** goes further, compelling a positive act — demolishing an encroaching wall, restoring a blocked rasta — and courts grant it more cautiously still.

What litigants call a "stay order" is almost always a temporary injunction or an interim order in that application.

## The three ingredients

Every temporary injunction application is tested against three cumulative requirements:

1. **Prima facie case.** Not proof — the suit is yet to be tried — but a serious, arguable claim shown from the plaint and documents. A plaintiff with a registered deed and possession has one; a plaintiff with a bare assertion rarely does.
2. **Balance of convenience.** Whom does the interim period hurt more? Restraining sale of disputed land costs the defendant little if he truly owns it; refusing restraint can cost the plaintiff the land itself to a bona fide purchaser.
3. **Irreparable loss.** Harm that money cannot adequately repair — loss of specific immovable property, demolition, destruction of a business — as opposed to a quantifiable debt.

All three must coexist. Courts also weigh conduct: concealment of material facts or approaching the court after long delay can sink an otherwise strong application, because an injunction is an **equitable, discretionary** remedy.

## Interim orders and the first hearing

Because notice to the other side takes time, courts can pass an **ex parte interim injunction** on day one where the urgency is genuine — but Order XXXIX Rule 3 expects reasons for dispensing with notice, and the order is then heard inter partes quickly. A common middle course in our district courts is the **status quo order**: both parties restrained from changing the existing position — no alienation, no construction, no dispossession — until the application is decided. Status quo orders are easier to obtain but notoriously litigated over what the "existing position" actually was, so have your possession evidence (fard, bills, photographs) ready on day one.

An injunction order, granted or refused, is appealable as an order under Order XLIII CPC, which is why injunction applications are often fought harder than the suit itself.

## Disobedience: the teeth

An injunction is a court command, and **Order XXXIX Rule 2(3) CPC** arms it: for breach, the court may order **attachment of the violator's property** and his **detention in civil prison**. Just as practically, courts can **undo acts done in violation** — a sale deed registered in the teeth of a restraint order, construction raised overnight — restoring the protected position. If the other side violates your injunction, move immediately with dated proof (photographs, registry searches, witnesses); delay reads as acquiescence.

## Using injunctions well — and defending against them

For plaintiffs: file early, disclose everything, and frame the restraint narrowly — courts grant surgical orders more readily than sweeping ones. For defendants: attack the weakest ingredient, offer undertakings that neutralise the alleged urgency, and seek costs or security where the injunction is a pressure tactic. Courts can require the applicant to furnish security precisely so that a wrongly obtained injunction has a price.

Whether you need an injunction or need one lifted, the outcome usually turns on the first hearing and the documents attached that day. A brief consultation with your title papers, possession evidence and the timeline of the threat settles both the strategy and the drafting before that hearing arrives.
