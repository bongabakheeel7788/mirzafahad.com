Most tenancies in Punjab begin on a hundred-rupee stamp paper bought in five minutes outside katchery, signed without reading, never registered. Both sides then discover, mid-dispute, that the document answers none of the questions that matter: who pays for the motor repair, when rent increases, what the advance covers, how either side exits. The Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009 provides the frame; a well-drafted agreement fills it.

## What the Act requires

The Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009 governs urban residential and commercial tenancies in the province. Its core demands are simple: the tenancy should be **in writing**, and the agreement should be **registered with the Rent Registrar** appointed for the area — a quick, low-cost step both parties routinely skip. Registration matters asymmetrically in practice: a registered agreement is the clean evidentiary foundation for everything a landlord or tenant later needs from the Rent Tribunal, while an unregistered arrangement leaves whoever needs to prove the tenancy's terms improvising with rent receipts and witnesses.

The Act also created **Rent Tribunals** (special judges for rent cases) with exclusive jurisdiction over eviction and related disputes, replacing the slower general civil track for these matters.

## The clauses that actually get fought over

Draft around tomorrow's dispute, not today's goodwill:

- **Parties and premises**, precisely — CNIC numbers, complete address, what is included (upper portion, garage, rooftop rights, fixtures).
- **Rent, due date and mode.** Specify payment by bank transfer where possible; a banking trail ends most "he never paid / I always paid cash" disputes before they start.
- **Annual increase.** State the percentage and date it applies. Where an agreement is silent, the Act supplies a default annual increase mechanism — better to choose your own figure than inherit one.
- **Term.** A fixed term with a stated renewal procedure. Expiry of the agreed period is itself a ground of eviction, which cuts both ways: tenants wanting stability should negotiate longer terms.
- **Advance and security.** Record the exact amount, that it is refundable, permitted deductions (unpaid utilities, damage beyond wear and tear), and a refund deadline after vacation. This is the single most litigated paragraph in tenancies.
- **Utilities and taxes.** Who pays electricity, gas, water and property tax, and the obligation to hand over paid bills at exit — unpaid utility arrears discovered after handover are a classic trap.
- **Repairs.** Structural repairs to the landlord, day-to-day maintenance to the tenant, is the usual allocation; write it rather than assume it.
- **Subletting and use.** Prohibit or permit subletting expressly, and state the permitted use (residential only, or the specific business) — unauthorised subletting and change of use are eviction grounds.

## Eviction: how it lawfully happens

A landlord cannot evict by changing locks, cutting the water or sending persuasive visitors — self-help eviction exposes the landlord to criminal and civil consequences. The lawful route is an **eviction petition before the Rent Tribunal** on the Act's grounds: **default in payment of rent**, **expiry of the tenancy period**, **subletting or change of use without consent**, **personal bona fide requirement** of the landlord or family, or the building's **required reconstruction**. The Act's procedure is designed to be faster than an ordinary suit, and it contains a sharp discipline for tenants: during proceedings the tenant must keep depositing rent as directed, and default in those deposits can collapse the defence itself.

Tenants should know the mirror image: a tenant paying rent on time, holding receipts or bank records, and within the agreed term, is hard to evict — the Act protects possession that honours its own terms.

## Habits for both sides

Landlords: verify the tenant's CNIC, complete the police tenant-registration formality your district requires, and issue receipts or insist on bank payment — your eviction case one day will be built on this record. Tenants: never pay the advance before signing, photograph the premises' condition at entry, keep every receipt, and get the agreement registered — the party with the paper wins the Rent Tribunal's confidence.

An agreement reviewed before signature costs a fraction of one dispute under a bad one. A brief consultation with the draft — or with the receipts and correspondence once a dispute has started — settles the strategy on either side of the relationship.
