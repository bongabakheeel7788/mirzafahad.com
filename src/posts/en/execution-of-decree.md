Clients celebrate the decree; lawyers know the file is only half closed. A decree for money, possession or an injunction does not enforce itself — the judgment-debtor rarely walks in with a cheque. Enforcement happens through a separate proceeding called **execution**, governed mainly by **Order XXI CPC**, the longest Order in the Code for a reason: it is where resistance happens.

## Starting execution

Execution begins with an **execution petition** filed in the court that passed the decree, or a court to which the decree is transferred under **section 38 CPC** — typically where the judgment-debtor's property or person is. The petition states the decree, the amount or relief outstanding, and the **mode** of execution you seek. The court issues notice to the judgment-debtor in most cases, and his objections — often the first round of delay — are heard before coercive steps.

Do not sit on a decree. Fresh execution applications face an outer bar: as a working rule, **six years** from the decree under section 48 CPC, with limitation rules governing each application in between. Interest and costs keep accruing per the decree's terms, but a stale decree invites every technical objection.

## The modes of execution

Order XXI offers a toolbox; the right tool depends on the decree:

- **Attachment and sale of property.** Movable property can be seized; immovable property is attached, then sold by **court auction**. The process has mandatory stages — proclamation of sale, valuation, publication, the auction itself, confirmation of sale — and objections are possible at nearly every one. Certain property is exempt from attachment under section 60 CPC: necessary wearing apparel, tools of artisans, and for agriculturists their implements and such land as the law protects.
- **Possession warrants.** A decree for possession of immovable property is executed through a warrant under Order XXI; the bailiff delivers possession, with police help and even removal of obstruction if the court orders. Resistance by a third party claiming independent rights triggers its own mini-inquiry.
- **Arrest and detention.** A money decree can be enforced against the judgment-debtor's person under **sections 51 and 55 CPC** — civil imprisonment, after the court is satisfied he has means to pay and refuses or neglects to. Safeguards apply: notice to show cause is the norm, and **women cannot be arrested** in execution of a money decree (section 56).
- **Garnishee-type recovery.** Debts owed to the judgment-debtor — salary within legal limits, bank balances, rent — can be attached so the money comes to the court directly.
- **Injunctions and specific acts.** Disobedience of a decree for an injunction or specific performance can be enforced by attachment of property and detention, or the court can have the act done at the judgment-debtor's cost.

## Why execution takes time

Three honest reasons. First, **objections**: the judgment-debtor, his relatives and sometimes invented claimants file objection petitions — to the attachment, the valuation, the auction — each needing adjudication under Order XXI's own machinery (rules 58 onwards for third-party claims). Second, **process friction**: warrants must issue, bailiffs must go, auctions must be advertised; each step is a date on a crowded board. Third, the executing court **cannot go behind the decree** — it enforces what was decreed, no more — but defining exactly what was decreed (mesne profits, interest calculations, boundaries) generates its own disputes.

A realistic expectation in Punjab's district courts: a contested execution of a money or possession decree commonly runs months to a couple of years, faster where the asset is identified early and objections are weak.

## Making execution faster

- **Identify assets before you sue.** The best execution strategy is set at plaint stage: attachment before judgment (Order XXXVIII) where the defendant is dissipating assets.
- **Ask for precise decrees.** A decree with exact amounts, khasra numbers and boundaries executes; a vague one litigates.
- **Pursue every date.** Execution files die of inattention more than of law.
- **Use arrest sparingly but credibly.** The genuine prospect of civil detention settles more money decrees than auctions do.

If you hold a decree that has not turned into money or possession, a brief consultation with the decree sheet, the judgment and whatever you know of the debtor's assets will usually produce a concrete execution plan — which mode, which court, and in what order.
