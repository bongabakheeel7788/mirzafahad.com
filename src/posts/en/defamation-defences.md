Every defamation case is really two cases: the claimant's, that reputation was wrongly injured, and the defendant's, that the law protects what was said. The defences are where publishers, reviewers, journalists, complainants and ordinary posters either stand or fall — and each defence has machinery that popular understanding gets wrong.

## Truth (justification)

Substantial truth defeats a civil claim: the law compensates injury to the reputation one *deserves*, not the reputation one has. Three practical refinements matter. First, **the burden is the defendant's** — you prove truth, the claimant does not prove falsity — and it must be proved to the civil standard with admissible evidence, not community conviction. Second, **substantial truth suffices**: minor inaccuracies that leave the sting accurate do not destroy the defence. Third, criminal defamation adds a filter: under the PPC's first exception, truth defends only where publication was **for the public good** — a genuine constraint on using true private facts as a weapon.

The practical rule for anyone publishing accusations: hold the evidence *before* publishing, in a form a court could receive — documents, records, first-hand witnesses — or hold the post.

## Fair comment

Democracy's defence: honest **opinion** on a matter of **public interest**, based on **facts truly stated or sufficiently indicated**. It shelters the harsh restaurant review, criticism of public projects, analysis of a public figure's performance. Its boundaries are policed at three points: the comment must be recognisable as opinion rather than a factual allegation ("the food was dreadful" versus "the kitchen uses expired stock" — the second is a fact you must prove); the underlying facts must be true; and the opinion must be honestly held, not a vehicle for malice. Writers protect themselves structurally — state the true facts, then comment on them visibly as evaluation.

## Privilege

**Absolute privilege** immunises statements where candour matters more than reputation: judicial proceedings (pleadings, testimony, arguments), legislative proceedings, and certain official acts. A witness cannot be sued in defamation for testimony; the remedy for lying under oath is perjury, not libel.

**Qualified privilege** protects good-faith communications made under legal, social or moral **duty to a person with a corresponding interest**: reporting suspected crime to police, an employer's reference, a complaint to a regulator, warnings within a family about a proposed transaction. Two limits define it: the communication must stay within the duty channel (the police complaint is privileged; posting the same allegations publicly is not), and **malice destroys it** — proof that the maker knew the statement false or acted from spite forfeits the protection. This is the defence that protects genuine complainants, and the reason "he reported me falsely to the authorities" cases turn on proving bad faith rather than mere falsity.

## Consent, and the practical hierarchy

Consent to publication defeats a claim, and fair and accurate **reports of court proceedings** carry their own protection — the shield of court reporters.

When counsel triages an accusation of defamation, the analysis runs in order: was it in a privileged forum? Is it provably true — and if criminal, for public good? Is it comment on true facts honestly held? Did the claimant consent? A publisher who can answer yes at any tier has a case; one who answers no down the list has a settlement to negotiate — early, for the reasons the rest of this series explains.
