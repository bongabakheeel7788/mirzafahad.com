The Protection Against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act, 2010 gave Pakistan one of the region's earliest dedicated harassment frameworks, and the 2022 amendments substantially widened it. What it still lacks is use: most workplaces in Punjab's smaller cities have never constituted the committee the law requires, and most targets of harassment have never been told the machinery exists. This article is the operating manual.

## What the Act covers

The Act defines harassment around **unwelcome** conduct: sexual advances, demands for favours, verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature, and — critically for real workplaces — conduct that **interferes with work or creates an intimidating, hostile or offensive environment**, including the punishment dimension where refusal is met with professional retaliation. The 2022 amendments broadened the frame further — extending protective scope in gender terms, reaching **students, domestic workers and less formal work relationships**, and widening "workplace" to match how work actually happens, transport and virtual spaces included. The complainant's employment status is read generously; the Act was written to be hard to escape on definitional technicalities.

## The two forums

**The inquiry committee.** Every organisation is required to constitute a three-member committee (with prescribed composition including women's representation) to receive complaints, inquire on defined timelines, and recommend penalties — from censure through dismissal — that the employer implements. The committee route suits functioning organisations where the accused does not control the machinery.

**The Ombudsperson.** Each province (and the federation) maintains an **Ombudsperson for protection against harassment** — in Punjab, a standing office receiving complaints directly. This route matters precisely where the internal route fails: no committee constituted, the accused is the boss, or the committee's independence is theatre. The Ombudsperson inquires with real powers, and orders flow with appeal routes above. Where an organisation simply has no committee, that failure is itself a violation attracting consequences — worth stating in the complaint.

Timelines matter: complaints should move promptly after incidents, and the machinery's own deadlines are comparatively tight by Pakistani standards — the design intent was months, not years.

## Building the complaint

Harassment cases are pattern cases. The file: **dates, places, exact words and acts** in a contemporaneous private log; **messages preserved** (the medium of most modern harassment — never delete, even when disgusted); **witnesses** to incidents or to your contemporaneous distress (colleagues told at the time become corroboration); and any **prior complaints** by others about the same person, which committees and Ombudspersons rightly weigh. The complaint itself should be specific, chronological and factual — the register of a professional stating events, which is exactly what tribunals credit.

## Retaliation, confidentiality and the accused

Retaliation — termination, transfer-as-punishment, hostile marking — is itself actionable within the machinery, and documented retaliation has repeatedly become the strongest part of complainants' cases; log it as it happens. Proceedings carry confidentiality protections, and false-complaint provisions exist symmetrically — a reminder that the Act protects truthful complaints robustly and manufactured ones not at all.

For the accused: the process is a formal inquiry with a right to respond — engage it seriously, in writing, with counsel's guidance; and for **employers**, the checklist is short and statutory: constitute the committee, display the code, train it, and treat the first complaint as the test your organisation will be judged by — because under the amended Act, it will be.
