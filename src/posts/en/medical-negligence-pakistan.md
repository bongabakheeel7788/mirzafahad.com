Between the grief of a treatment gone wrong and the wall of institutional silence that follows it, Pakistani families historically had two options: the mob outside the ward, or nothing. The last fifteen years quietly built a third: a functioning complaint architecture that investigates, sanctions and — through the courts — compensates. It rewards precisely what grief makes hardest: method.

## Negligence versus misfortune

The legal line first, because it disciplines everything after: medicine's bad outcomes divide into **inherent risk materialising despite proper care** — not actionable — and **care below the competent standard causing harm** — actionable. The claimant's case is always two links: substandard care (measured against what a reasonably competent practitioner in that field would have done — the classic professional-standard test) and **causation** (that failure, not the disease, produced the harm). Missed diagnoses despite proper workup, disclosed complications occurring without fault, and honest judgment calls between accepted options generally fall on the misfortune side; wrong-site surgery, unattended emergencies, forged qualifications, transfusion and dosage errors, and abandonment mid-treatment populate the negligence side.

## The complaint architecture

**Punjab Healthcare Commission (PHC).** The province's statutory regulator of healthcare establishments receives complaints of maladministration and failures of care — free, on documents, without needing a lawyer — investigates through expert panels, and wields real sanctions: fines, directions, and closure powers against establishments including the quackery it campaigns against. For most families this is the primary forum: it establishes an expert-backed factual record that every other route can then use.

**The practitioners' regulator.** The medical and dental regulatory framework (the PMDC in its current statutory form) addresses the **licence**: professional misconduct proceedings against the individual doctor, with penalties to suspension and removal from the register.

**Civil compensation.** Damages for negligent treatment run through the civil courts — and consumer forums have entertained medical-service deficiencies — with the PHC/expert record as spine, quantifying treatment costs, lost earnings and dependency on the principles our accident-compensation article describes.

**Criminal law** stays available for the gross end — rash or grossly negligent acts causing death engage the criminal negligence provisions — but as our FAQ notes, it is usually the wrong first move: hardest to prove, most resisted, and least productive of either truth or compensation compared to the expert routes.

## Building the case

Everything turns on records obtained **early**: the complete medical file (admission notes, operative notes, prescriptions, monitoring charts — request formally in writing; alteration of records after a demand is itself damning and investigators know to look), the medicines and their batch evidence, second-opinion documentation, and in death cases the difficult but often decisive question of **post-mortem examination**. Chronology written by the family while fresh — who said what, when, which doctor attended at which hour — routinely supplies the thread expert panels pull.

## Honest expectations

The system's real outputs: PHC findings and sanctions arrive on a scale of months and carry institutional weight; licence action against individuals is real but conservative; civil compensation is achievable with expert-backed causation but tests patience. What no forum awards is what families most want — the acknowledgment freely given. The method's consolation is narrower but real: an expert record that names what happened, consequences that change behaviour, and compensation that meets at least the material part of the loss. It is more than the mob outside the ward ever obtained — and it leaves the family, unlike the mob, on the right side of every subsequent proceeding.
