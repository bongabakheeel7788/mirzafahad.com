A bill triple the usual amount, or a "detection bill" alleging you stole units months ago, lands with a short due date and the implied threat of disconnection. Most people either pay in despair or stop paying altogether — one wastes money, the other risks losing the connection. The law gives you a middle path: contest in writing, pay strategically, and climb the forums in order.

## Why bills inflate

Common causes, each with a different fix: **estimated readings**, where the meter reader never came and an average was invented; **wrong meter reading or wrong slab**, where one extra digit pushes the whole bill into a higher tariff slab; **a defective or fast meter**; **arrears of someone else's default** transferred onto your reference; and **detection bills**, discussed below. Get the bill's snapshot photo (printed on modern bills) and compare it with your actual meter — this single check exposes a large share of errors.

## Detection bills and their legal limits

A detection bill charges you for allegedly unrecorded consumption — a tampered or slow meter, a bypassed connection. Under the NEPRA framework that binds every distribution company (the Consumer Service Manual made under the NEPRA Act 1997), a detection bill is not a blank cheque: it requires a documented basis, checking of the meter through the prescribed process, and it may only reach back a limited number of months depending on the consumer category. Bills computed on imagination, charged for long past periods, or issued without the meter ever being tested in the metering and testing laboratory are exactly the kind that get struck down. Demand, in writing, the basis of the detection: the checking report, the date of inspection, who signed it, and whether you were present or notified.

If theft is alleged seriously, be aware the Electricity Act framework and amendments criminalise power theft — which is one more reason to contest a false allegation formally rather than ignore it.

## The complaint ladder, in order

1. **Sub-divisional office (SDO) in writing.** Hand in a written application describing the error, attach the bill and your meter photo, and get a received stamp on your copy. Verbal visits achieve nothing provable.
2. **Revenue Officer / XEN of the division.** If the SDO does nothing before the due date, escalate with a copy of the stamped first application.
3. **Meter checking.** Ask for the meter to be checked, and for a disputed meter, testing in the M&T laboratory; you are entitled to notice of the test. A confirmed defective meter resets the billing to a proper basis.
4. **NEPRA.** The regulator runs its own complaint mechanism and regional offices, and distribution companies answer to it. A NEPRA complaint, filed online or in writing with your prior correspondence attached, moves files that local offices sat on.
5. **Electric Inspector.** Billing and metering disputes fall within the jurisdiction of the provincial Electric Inspector, a technical forum that can decide the dispute on its merits.
6. **Courts.** Consumer courts in Punjab have treated overbilling as a faulty service under the Punjab Consumer Protection Act 2005, and civil courts can grant declarations and injunctions against plainly illegal demands — including, in proper cases, a restraint on disconnection while the dispute is decided.

## Protecting the connection while you fight

Disconnection pressure is the company's real weapon. Blunt it: pay the **undisputed portion** — roughly your normal average — before the due date, in writing "under protest, without prejudice to my complaint No. ___". A consumer paying the admitted amount and contesting the balance through proper channels is in a strong position before every forum; a consumer in total default is not. Never pay anyone cash to "fix" a bill — corrections happen on the record or not at all.

## Keep a bill file

One folder with twelve months of bills, your complaint copies with receiving stamps, meter photos with dates, and any checking reports. Billing disputes are won by the side with the better paper trail, and the company's own record-keeping is often weaker than yours can be.

Whether your particular bill calls for an SDO application, a NEPRA complaint, the Electric Inspector or a stay from court depends on the amounts, the allegation and the stage — a short consultation with the bills and correspondence in hand settles the route quickly.
