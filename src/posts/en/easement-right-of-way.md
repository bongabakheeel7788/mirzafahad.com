Few disputes turn neighbours into enemies faster than a blocked path. A wall rises overnight across the track your tractor has used for thirty years; a plot buyer discovers his parcel has no legal access at all; a village street quietly becomes someone's cattle yard. The law of easements is old, technical and — used properly — decisively effective.

## What an easement is

An easement is a right enjoyed over another person's land for the beneficial use of your own: a right of way, a right to light, to flow of water, to drainage. The land enjoying the right is the dominant tenement; the land burdened is the servient tenement. The **Easements Act 1882** supplies the framework courts apply across Punjab, alongside revenue law for recorded village paths.

The point parties misunderstand most: an easement is a right over *someone else's* property. You do not own the path — you own the right to pass along it, and the servient owner keeps everything consistent with that right.

## The three main ways a right of way arises

1. **Easement of necessity.** When land is split — by sale, partition or inheritance — and a parcel is left with no access except over the remaining land, the law implies a right of way of necessity. It lasts while the necessity lasts and dies if you later acquire alternative access. This is the plot-buyer's lifeline: a landlocked parcel is not a worthless one.
2. **Prescription.** Use of a way that has been **open, peaceful, as of right (without permission) and continuous for about twenty years** matures into a prescriptive easement under the Act. Each adjective matters: use by the owner's permission, however old, never prescribes; neither does secret or contested use. Village witnesses, girdawari entries and old photographs become the evidence.
3. **Grant or agreement.** A right of way created by document — in a sale deed, partition deed or separate agreement — is the cleanest form. When buying any interior plot, insist the access is written into the deed, with width and location specified.

Recorded **village rastas** are a fourth, practical category: paths shown in the shajra and revenue record, serving the village or a group of holdings. Their record entries carry strong weight, and the revenue hierarchy itself can proceed against encroachment on recorded common paths.

## When the path is blocked: remedies

The toolkit, usually combined in one civil suit:

- **Declaration** of your easement — necessity, prescription or grant — under the Specific Relief Act.
- **Mandatory injunction** directing removal of the wall, gate or obstruction.
- **Permanent injunction** restraining future interference.
- **Interim (temporary) injunction** at the suit's outset, which is where the real fight happens: courts can restore passage pending trial where you show a prima facie case, balance of convenience and irreparable harm — and for a farmer cut off from his fields in sowing season, that showing is usually makeable.
- **Damages** for proven loss — the crop that rotted, the construction halted.

Where the obstruction is on a recorded public or village path, complaints to the revenue authorities run in parallel, and criminal law occasionally enters through obstruction and mischief provisions — though the civil suit is where title to the right is settled.

## What wins and loses these cases

Winning evidence: the shajra showing the path; old revenue entries; the partition or sale deed chain showing how the parcels divided; consistent witness testimony of decades of open use; photographs over time; proof there is no alternative access. Losing positions: use that began with permission; a claimed path that wanders year to year; demanding a new, more convenient route rather than the historic one (necessity gives you *a* way, not your preferred way); and sleeping on the obstruction for years before suing.

For the servient owner, the defences mirror these: permission, interruption, abandonment, alternative access, or that the claimed user never existed as of right.

## Before it reaches court

Many rasta disputes settle at the village level once the record is actually examined — parties often argue from memory about a path the shajra settles in one look. Obtain the fard and shajra extract, photograph the obstruction and the ground, and note the dates. A brief consultation with those documents in hand usually settles whether you hold a right worth suing on, which remedy fits, and whether a firm notice will reopen the path without a suit at all.
