"I can't afford a lawyer" ends more meritorious claims in Pakistan than any point of law. Yet a real, if patchy, legal-aid infrastructure exists — statutory, judicial, professional and charitable. Knowing the doors and how to knock is itself half the access.

## The statutory system: LAJA

The **Legal Aid and Justice Authority Act, 2020** created a federal Legal Aid and Justice Authority to provide legal aid — counsel, advice and assistance — to the poor in criminal cases, with an expanding footprint and panels of paid lawyers. Deservingness turns on financial inability, assessed through its application process. It is the formal front door for indigent accused persons, and its reach has been growing case by case and district by district.

## The judiciary's own funds

Punjab's district judiciary administers **legal-empowerment/legal-aid funds** through district committees — chaired at the sessions level — which can engage counsel at state expense for deserving litigants, most establishedly for indigent accused in serious criminal trials (where the right to counsel at state expense in capital cases is long recognised in the rules) and increasingly through committee schemes for civil and family litigants of proven means-lessness. The application route is practical: through the **Sessions Judge's office/district legal aid committee**, with a means declaration; court staff and any local lawyer can direct you to the current committee's process.

Family courts add their own softener: their procedures are simplified by design, court fees are nominal in most family claims, and judges assist unrepresented women more actively than in ordinary civil litigation — imperfect, but real.

## Bar associations and pro bono

Every district bar, Faisalabad and Tandlianwala included, maintains **free legal aid committees** in some form, and individual advocates take deserving cases without fee more often than the profession's reputation suggests — particularly for widows, prisoners and clear injustices. The effective approach is specific and documented: arrive with your papers and a one-page summary; a request that shows its own merit finds volunteers faster than a general plea for help.

## Specialised and charitable channels

For **women**: Punjab's violence-against-women and Dar-ul-Aman ecosystems connect residents to legal help; the **Punjab Women's Helpline (1043)** routes legal queries; and women's-rights organisations — legal-aid NGOs active in Punjab — run counsel panels for family, inheritance and violence cases. For **prisoners**: jail visits by district legal aid committees and NGO programs reach the unrepresented; families of detainees should ask the jail's welfare officer about aid panels. For **workers**: labour unions and some labour-court practitioners run assistance schemes. For **overseas Pakistanis' families**: OPF's complaint channels include legal facilitation. Quality varies across all of these — but so does it at the paid bar.

## Making any of it work

Whatever the door: prepare a **one-page case summary** (parties, dates, what happened, what you seek), photocopy your documents into an orderly set, obtain your means evidence (income affidavit, utility bills — committees ask), and follow up in writing with names and dates. Aid systems ration by persistence; the applicant with a clean file and polite tenacity is the one the system serves.

And one caution: "free help" offered by courthouse strangers, with your original documents requested, is the fraud economy — real legal aid never needs your originals to begin, and never asks for "file charges" in cash to unknown persons.

If your matter reaches this chamber and genuine hardship exists, say so plainly at the first consultation. Like most colleagues, cases are taken every year where the fee is adjusted, deferred — or waived. The profession's oldest tradition is older than its reputation.
