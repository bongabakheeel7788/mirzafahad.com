Every month, a few hundred rupees leave the books of registered employers across Pakistan for a fund most workers never think about — until they turn sixty and discover it owes them a pension for life. The Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution (EOBI) runs the only national pension scheme for private-sector workers, and both the entitlement and the common ways employers defeat it are worth understanding years before retirement.

## Who the scheme covers

The **Employees' Old-Age Benefits Act 1976** applies to industrial and commercial establishments above a small employee threshold, and coverage has been extended progressively over the years. In a covered establishment, every employee — permanent, contractual or through a contractor — is an "insured person" once registered.

Contributions are the employer's burden: the employer pays a percentage of the minimum wage per worker, and a much smaller share is deducted from the employee. The amounts are pegged to the minimum wage and change with it, so check current figures rather than relying on any article. The key point is that **registration and payment are the employer's legal duties**, enforced by EOBI's own inspection and recovery machinery.

## The benefits

EOBI pays four things:

- **Old-age pension** — a monthly pension for life, from age **sixty for men and fifty-five for women** (earlier for mine workers), after a minimum of **fifteen years of insurable employment**.
- **Reduced early pension** — up to five years before pension age, at a permanently reduced rate.
- **Invalidity pension** — on permanent incapacity for work, with a shorter qualifying period.
- **Survivors' pension** — on the death of the insured person or pensioner, payable to the widow or widower for life, with the law extending benefits to children and, in defined cases, parents. Where death occurs in service, a short minimum contribution period (a few years) suffices.

Workers with **less than fifteen years** do not get a monthly pension but may receive a one-time **old-age grant** based on their contributions. The minimum pension amount is revised by the federal government from time to time — again, check the current figure.

## The classic problem: the unregistered worker

The scheme's weak point is enforcement. Thousands of factories and shops in Faisalabad's industrial belt either never register, register only part of their workforce, or register workers at wages lower than reality. The worker discovers the gap at sixty, when decades of service show only a few years of contributions.

Three things protect you:

1. **Get your EOBI registration card / insured person number early.** Ask HR directly. Registration can be done online, and the worker can verify status through EOBI's facilitation system using CNIC.
2. **Check your contribution record periodically** — EOBI provides individual contribution information through its offices and online facilitation. A missing year is far easier to fix while the employer still exists and the record is fresh.
3. **Keep employment proof.** Appointment letters, salary slips, bank credits, social security cards, attendance records. If the employer never paid, EOBI can assess and recover contributions from the employer retrospectively — your evidence of employment drives that assessment, and courts have repeatedly held that the worker should not suffer for the employer's default.

## Claiming the pension

The claim is filed at the EOBI regional office with the insured person's card or number, CNIC, employer details and service proof; survivors add the death certificate and proof of relationship (NADRA family registration certificate). Pensions are now paid through bank accounts. Where EOBI rejects or underpays a claim — usually on grounds of insufficient contribution record — the dispute goes first through EOBI's internal adjudication, with appeals lying onward as the Act provides; many disputed claims succeed once the missing employment period is proven.

Two practical warnings. First, **do not buy "EOBI agents'" promises** — the process needs no middleman, and fake facilitators charging to "arrange" pensions are a recurring fraud. Second, widows frequently fail to claim the survivors' pension at all, not knowing it exists; if your late spouse ever worked in a registered factory or company, it costs nothing to have the record checked.

If retirement is approaching and your contribution record is short of what your service deserves, do not wait for the claim stage to fight that battle. A brief consultation with your CNIC, employment proof and whatever EOBI record you can print settles whether to pursue the employer, EOBI, or both — and in which order.
