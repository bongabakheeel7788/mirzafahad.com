"He ate my money" is probably the most common sentence spoken in police stations across Punjab, and the two sections it summons — 406 and 420 PPC — are also the two most misused. One of them punishes a trust betrayed; the other punishes a lie told at the outset. Knowing which is which decides whether an FIR will survive, whether bail is routine, and whether the dispute really belongs in a civil court instead.

## Criminal breach of trust: sections 405 and 406

Section 405 defines the offence; section 406 punishes it with up to seven years' imprisonment and fine. The ingredients are precise:

1. **Entrustment** — property (money, goods, documents) was handed to the accused, or he had dominion over it, in a fiduciary way: an agent holding sale proceeds, a committee (BC) organiser holding members' contributions, a transporter carrying goods, a shopkeeper holding articles for safe custody.
2. **Dishonest misappropriation or conversion** — the accused used the property as his own, disposed of it, or refused to return it, in violation of the terms of the entrustment.

The property arrives **lawfully**; the crime is in what happens afterwards. Aggravated forms exist for carriers (407), clerks and servants (408), and public servants, bankers, merchants and agents (409), with heavier ceilings.

## Cheating: sections 415 and 420

Section 415 defines cheating; 420 punishes its aggravated form — cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property — with up to seven years and fine. Here the timeline is reversed: the accused, **from the beginning**, deceives the victim and by that deception induces him to deliver property or alter a valuable security. The classic patterns: selling the same plot to two buyers, taking advance payment for goods never intended to be supplied, fake overseas-employment promises, and bogus investment schemes promising monthly profit.

The hinge is **intention at the moment of the promise**. If the accused genuinely meant to perform and later failed — the business sank, the goods were seized, the market collapsed — there is no cheating, only a civil claim for the money.

## The misuse problem

Every trial court in Faisalabad sees it weekly: a partnership gone sour, an unpaid invoice, a committee that collapsed, re-drafted as an FIR under 406/420 to pressure the other side into paying. Superior courts have said repeatedly that criminal process must not be used as a recovery agency, and they quash such FIRs or acquit under sections 249-A / 265-K CrPC where the dispute is patently civil. Indicators courts look for:

- A written agreement with performance spread over time (suggests contract, not crime).
- Running accounts between the parties with mutual payments.
- The complainant's own delay — years of silence, then an FIR timed with a civil suit.
- No specific act of deception alleged, just "he did not pay."

For a genuine victim, the mirror lesson: your FIR must allege the **specific false representation**, its date, the payment it induced, and why the intention was dishonest from the start — otherwise it reads as a civil dispute and dies early.

## Bail and the usual course of these cases

With a seven-year maximum, both offences sit outside the prohibitory clause of section 497 CrPC, so pre-arrest and post-arrest bail are granted in the ordinary course unless the accused absconds, tampers with evidence, or faces a string of similar FIRs. Investigations centre on documents — receipts, cheques, bank statements, WhatsApp admissions — far more than witnesses. Many cases end in compromise: both sections are compoundable with the court's permission, and repayment against withdrawal is the commonest ending.

## Defending a 406/420 FIR

- Collect the **entire** financial trail, not just favourable slices — courts distrust selective ledgers.
- If the dispute is contractual, move early: a petition to quash, or an application under 249-A/265-K, framed on the civil nature of the matter.
- Never make partial payments through back channels without documentation; undocumented payments vanish when the complainant denies them.
- Where the complainant has also filed a civil suit on the same facts, place it on the criminal record — parallel civil proceedings powerfully support the civil-dispute defence.

Whether you are the one who paid or the one accused, the case turns on documents and on the timeline of intention. A brief consultation with the agreement, receipts and bank record in hand usually settles whether this is a criminal prosecution, a civil recovery suit, or a compromise waiting to be drafted.
