A man has three sons. One son dies in his father's lifetime, leaving children. When the grandfather later dies — do those grandchildren get their late father's share? Under classical doctrine, largely no: the nearer surviving sons exclude the deceased son's children, whose provision was left to a discretionary bequest. Pakistan changed this by statute, and the change matters in thousands of families.

## Section 4 of the MFLO

Section 4 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961 provides that where a son or daughter of the propositus (the deceased) died before the opening of succession, the **children of that predeceased child receive, per stirpes, the share which their parent would have received if alive**.

In the example: the estate is notionally divided among three sons; the late son's one-third passes to *his* children (divided among them by the usual double-share-to-sons principle), alongside their uncles' shares. The grandchildren stand in their father's shoes.

## Points the case law has settled

- **Daughters' children are covered.** The section speaks of children of a predeceased *son or daughter* — a predeceased daughter's children take the share she would have taken.
- **Per stirpes, not per capita.** The grandchildren collectively take their parent's share; they do not each count as a child of the grandfather.
- **The share is "as if alive".** The predeceased child is notionally resurrected for the arithmetic — with everything that implies for the other sharers' fractions.
- **It applies to succession, not gifts.** Section 4 governs devolution on death; it does not restrain the grandfather's lifetime dispositions.

## The unresolved friction

Section 4 has always sat uneasily beside classical doctrine, and religious scholarship criticises it; the Federal Shariat Court's engagement with it has generated litigation and commentary over the years. Practitioners track this closely — but as matters stand in the civil courts and revenue record, **section 4 is the operative law of Pakistan**, applied in mutations and succession certificates daily. Families should neither assume the classical exclusion nor rely on rumours of the section's demise; they should record the entitlement while the law provides it, promptly.

## The practical fights

**Omission at mutation.** The uncles process the grandfather's inheritance mutation listing only themselves — the predeceased brother's children, often young and living with their widowed mother, are left out. As with all omitted-heir cases, the remedy is a declaratory suit and correction; possession by co-heirs is not adverse to the omitted, and knowledge triggers the clock. The widowed mother should act as the children's next friend without delay — and where she needs authority over their shares, a guardianship-of-property appointment.

**Pressure to relinquish.** The orphaned family, dependent on the uncles, is pressed to release the share "since the grandfather supported you all these years". Support given is not consideration for inheritance surrendered; releases in these dependency dynamics attract the same judicial suspicion as women's relinquishments generally.

**Interaction with a will.** A grandfather can *still* make a will for orphaned grandchildren — useful in edge cases (step-relationships, adopted arrangements, foreign-law complications) — but where section 4 applies, the grandchildren inherit as of right; no will is needed and none should be demanded of anyone.

## The takeaway

If your parent died before your grandparent, you are not at your uncles' mercy: Pakistani law gives you your parent's share. Get the family record, check the mutation, and assert the entitlement early — this is one area where the statute is squarely on the orphan's side, and delay is the only thing that erodes it.
