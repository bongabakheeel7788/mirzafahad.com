"Company policy" is the phrase Pakistani consumers hear most when a product fails — as if a shop's signboard outranked provincial law. It does not. Here is what your money actually bought you in legal terms, and the ladder to climb when a seller refuses to honour it.

## The rights underneath the receipt

Under the consumer protection framework, goods must conform to their **description and the claims made for them**, be of proper quality, and be fit for their evident purpose. A product that fails these standards within its reasonable life — not merely within a stingy "7-day checking warranty" — entitles the consumer to remedies running from repair to replacement to **refund with damages** for consequential loss. Express **warranties** add to these rights; they do not replace them, and a warranty card's conditions cannot lawfully hollow out the statute.

Two trader obligations worth knowing because their breach strengthens every claim: sellers are required to **issue receipts**, and product claims — in advertising, on packaging, in an online listing — legally count. The listing that promised "original, PTA-approved, one-year warranty" is evidence, not decoration.

## Build the file before the fight

Whatever the product: keep the receipt or transaction proof, the box and its serial/batch identifiers, the warranty card, and — for online orders — screenshots of the **listing as it appeared when you ordered**, since listings mutate after disputes begin. When the defect appears, document it immediately: photos, videos of the malfunction, and where practical an independent workshop or expert note. The consumer with a file negotiates; the consumer with a story pleads.

## The escalation ladder

**Step one — the seller, in writing.** WhatsApp to the shop's business number or email stating the defect and the remedy sought. Polite, dated, specific. Much resolves here once the seller sees documentation rather than emotion.

**Step two — the brand.** For branded goods, the manufacturer's helpline and warranty network exist parallel to the seller; log a complaint number and keep it. Brands protect reputations shops don't have to.

**Step three — the statutory 15-day notice.** The formal notice under the Consumer Protection Act (see our consumer-court guide) — the step that changes the conversation, because it is the visible doorstep of litigation.

**Step four — the consumer court**, claiming refund and the losses the defect caused. For online marketplaces, join the platform where it made the sale or holds the payment; platform dispute systems, pressed in parallel, sometimes refund faster than the seller ever would.

## The recurring traps

**The "checking warranty".** A seven-day exchange window is a store courtesy, not the boundary of your rights over a product that dies in month two. **The repair loop** — endless "we'll repair it" cycles designed to exhaust the claimant: after reasonable repair attempts fail, the remedy conversation lawfully moves to replacement or refund; say so in writing. **The COD inspection myth** — refusing delivery inspection then denying claims: open and film unboxings for valuable orders. **The imported/no-warranty disclaimer** — the seller who sold it in Pakistan answers for it in Pakistan.

Persistence, in writing, wins the overwhelming majority of genuine defect claims before any judge is involved — because for the seller, your documented claim is a business cost calculation, and the file you built makes fighting more expensive than the refund.
