Every land dispute this office sees in Tandlianwala and Faisalabad has one document at its centre: the fard. Buyers who never read it, sellers who quietly sold more share than they owned, heirs who discovered a mutation they never knew about — the fard would have told each of them the truth in time. Understanding this one page is the cheapest legal protection a property buyer in Punjab can get.

## What the fard actually is

The fard malkiat is an extract from the record of rights maintained under the **Land Revenue Act 1967**. It is not a title deed; it is the revenue department's running statement of who owns what share in a given khewat, who possesses or cultivates it, and what entries — mutations, mortgages, court attachments — currently sit against it. Since computerisation under the **Punjab Land Records Authority (PLRA)**, the old patwari's hand-written register has been replaced for most of Punjab by a digital record served through Arazi Record Centres.

The law gives entries in the record of rights a presumption of truth. That presumption is rebuttable — courts go behind a fraudulent or mistaken entry regularly — but in practice the fard is where every transaction, every bank loan against land, and every inheritance begins.

## Khewat, khatooni, khasra — the three numbers

A fard is unreadable until you know its three spines:

- **Khewat** — the ownership account. One khewat may have many co-owners, each holding a fractional share (written like 1/4 or in traditional units). Your seller owns only his fraction, nothing more.
- **Khatooni** — the holding or cultivation account inside a khewat, showing who actually possesses or tills which portion. Owner and possessor are often different people, and that difference is where disputes grow.
- **Khasra** — the survey number of the physical parcel on the village map (shajra). The khasra ties the paper share to ground you can stand on.

Read the share column carefully. If the khewat totals 100 kanals among five owners and your seller's share computes to 12 kanals, no document he signs can convey you 20.

## How to obtain one

1. **Arazi Record Centre (ARC):** go to any ARC — they operate at tehsil level across Punjab, including Tandlianwala — with your original CNIC. After biometric verification, the service centre official traces the khewat by owner name, CNIC, or khasra number and issues a certified computerised fard the same visit. A fee applies; it is revised from time to time, so check the current schedule.
2. **Online:** PLRA offers online fard requests and record verification through its web portal, useful for overseas Pakistanis who want to see the record before travelling. For a transaction, however, the certified fard — and for a sale, the **fard brai farokht** (fard for sale) issued for the transfer process — is what the sub-registrar side of the transaction will work from.

## What a buyer must check before paying

Treat the fard as a checklist, not a formality:

- **Seller's name and CNIC** match the ownership entry — not his father's name with an unattested inheritance mutation still pending.
- **Share arithmetic:** the fraction he owns covers the area he is selling.
- **Mutations column:** look for recent intiqals — a sale mutation to someone else last month ends the conversation.
- **Encumbrances:** mortgage entries (especially bank mortgages), attachment orders, or notes of pending litigation. Land under a subsisting mortgage can be sold, but you inherit the problem.
- **Possession reality:** the khatooni tells you who is recorded in possession; then visit the land. Paper and ground must agree.
- **Fresh date:** a fard from six months ago proves what the record said six months ago. Obtain one dated days — not months — before you pay.

## Where the fard misleads

The record of rights is only as honest as its last correct entry. Forged mutations, entries attested without notice to co-sharers, and inheritance mutations that skipped a daughter all exist in the system and get struck down in court years later. A fard also says nothing about unregistered agreements to sell floating against the property. This is why the fard check is the beginning of due diligence — paired with the registered deed chain, mutation history, and physical inspection — not the whole of it.

If you are buying, selling, or untangling an entry that looks wrong, a brief consultation with the fard, the shajra extract, and any deeds in hand usually settles the strategy in a single sitting — most of these problems are cheap to prevent and expensive to litigate.
