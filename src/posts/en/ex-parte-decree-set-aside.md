You discover — often from a bank, a buyer, or the revenue record — that a court has already decreed a case against you, and you never attended a single hearing. That is an ex parte decree: a judgment passed in your absence after the court concluded you were served and chose to stay away. It is a real, executable decree, but the Civil Procedure Code gives you a specific and fast-moving remedy to undo it.

## How an ex parte decree happens

When a defendant fails to appear despite summons, Order IX of the CPC lets the court proceed ex parte. The plaintiff leads evidence unopposed, and the court decrees the suit if that one-sided evidence makes out the claim. In practice the weak link is usually **service**: summons sent to an old address, delivered to a relative, pasted on a wall, or published in a newspaper you never read (substituted service under Order V Rule 20). Process servers' reports are routinely accepted at face value — until you challenge them.

## The remedy — Order IX Rule 13 CPC

The direct remedy is an application under **Order IX Rule 13 CPC** to the same court that passed the decree. You must establish one of two grounds:

1. **Summons was not duly served.** Defective address, service on someone not authorised, substituted service ordered without first exhausting ordinary modes, a forged or doubtful process-server report — all of these count. The court examines the service record (the *talbana* file) closely once you contest it.
2. **Sufficient cause prevented your appearance.** Illness, being abroad with proof, a counsel's genuine default, a misleading date — the cause must explain the absence credibly and cover the whole period of non-appearance. "I was busy" or "I did not think it was serious" fails.

## The 30-day limitation

Limitation is **30 days**. Where you were duly served, it runs from the date of the decree. Where you were not duly served, it runs from the date of your **knowledge** of the decree — which is why the first thing your lawyer will pin down is exactly how and when you learned of it, and the first document you should preserve is whatever revealed it (an execution notice, a mutation entry, a bank letter). Delay beyond 30 days needs a separate, well-supported application under section 5 of the Limitation Act, and condonation is discretionary, not routine.

## What the court can order

If satisfied, the court sets aside the decree and restores the suit — usually on **terms**: costs to the plaintiff, sometimes security, and a direction to file the written statement by a fixed date. Setting aside is not an acquittal on merits; the suit simply resumes. If execution has already begun — attachment, auction steps, possession — your lawyer will simultaneously seek a stay of execution, because the Rule 13 application alone does not automatically suspend the decree.

## The alternative routes

Order IX Rule 13 is not the only door. An **appeal** lies against an ex parte decree under section 96(2) CPC, useful where the decree is bad on merits even on the plaintiff's own evidence. And where the decree was obtained by **fraud, misrepresentation or want of jurisdiction** — fake service engineered deliberately, a suit filed at a concocted address — an application under **section 12(2) CPC** to the same court attacks the decree's validity itself. The right route depends on your facts; sometimes two are pursued together, though a decision on one can bar the other, so the sequencing is a counsel's call, not a guess.

## Protecting yourself going forward

Keep your addresses current in CNIC, bank and revenue records; a dispute's summons goes where the paper trail points. If you hear even informally that someone has filed a case, have the court record checked immediately — an appearance at the right time costs far less than a year of restoration litigation.

If an ex parte decree has surfaced against you, the clock is already running. A brief consultation with the decree copy, the service record and whatever document brought you the news is usually enough to pick the right remedy and file it in time.
