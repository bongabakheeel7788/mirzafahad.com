When a bank learns that a customer has died, it freezes the account. From that moment, no ATM card, no cheque signed before death, and no relative's pleading will move the money — not even a spouse who deposited half of it. Understanding the bank's legal position saves families months of frustration.

## Why banks freeze accounts

On death, the account balance becomes part of the deceased's estate and belongs to **all** legal heirs in their shares. The bank cannot know who the heirs are, cannot adjudicate their claims, and faces liability if it pays the wrong person. So it pays only against legal authority: a **succession certificate** (from NADRA or the court) naming the movable assets, or a court order.

Withdrawing money after a death using the deceased's card or cheque — a common panic move — is unlawful, discoverable from the death record, and creates liability to the other heirs. Do not do it, and do not let a relative do it.

## The release process

1. Inform the branch and obtain the exact account details and balances (the bank will confirm to legal heirs).
2. Obtain the **death certificate** (Union Council) and NADRA family records.
3. Obtain a **succession certificate** listing each account, certificate and locker — the NADRA route where heirs are agreed, the court route otherwise.
4. Present the certificate; the bank verifies and releases to the heirs named, in the shares stated, usually by pay orders or transfers to each heir.

Timelines: with agreed heirs and the NADRA route, families commonly complete everything within one to two months. Contested cases take as long as the contest.

## Joint accounts — read the mandate

A joint account's fate depends on its operating instructions. An "either or survivor" mandate lets the survivor operate the account — but banks in Pakistan frequently still restrain operations on notice of death, and legally the **deceased's beneficial share in the funds remains part of the estate**: survivorship of operation is not survivorship of ownership. A widow operating a joint account should understand that heirs of the deceased retain claims to his contribution. Getting this wrong causes bitter litigation between, typically, a second wife and children of the first marriage.

## Lockers

Lockers are sealed on death and opened only against succession authority, in the presence of the heirs (and often bank officials recording an inventory). Do not expect an informal opening, and treat locker contents — usually gold — as estate property divided like everything else.

## Provident funds, insurance and nominations

Employment dues, provident fund, and insurance proceeds each follow their own instruments — and here a crucial principle applies: under Pakistani law as settled by the superior courts, a **nominee is generally a trustee for the legal heirs**, not the owner of the proceeds. A brother nominated in a policy years before the deceased married does not defeat the widow and children; the money is distributed as inheritance. There are scheme-specific nuances, but no heir should accept "there was a nominee" as the end of the conversation.

## Practical advice for account holders (before death)

Keep the family informed of where accounts exist; ensure CNIC-linked records (NADRA family data) are complete — undocumented children complicate certificates; consider consolidating scattered small accounts; and if you wish specific provision for someone, do it by lifetime transfer or a valid will within the one-third — not by assuming a nomination will suffice.

For heirs: move early, move together, and put every release through the certificate process. Money released properly is unchallengeable; money released cleverly follows the family into court for years.
