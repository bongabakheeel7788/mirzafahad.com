The nikahnama is the most important contract most Pakistanis ever sign, and the least read. It is filled in a noisy room, in minutes, by a nikah registrar following habit — and its blanks and crossed lines then govern rights for decades. Every column of the standard form exists for a reason. These are the ones that decide later disputes.

## Columns 13–16: the dower (mehr)

These columns record the amount of dower, how much is **prompt** (mu'ajjal — payable on demand, any time) and how much **deferred** (mu'wajjal — payable on divorce or death), and whether any was paid at the marriage. Three practical truths:

- An unspecified division is resolved by law and custom, but specifying it removes the argument. Prompt dower unpaid entitles the wife to refuse cohabitation; deferred dower becomes a debt on divorce or the husband's death — with priority against his estate.
- **Property can be dower.** A house or plot named as dower in these columns transfers real rights and is enforceable through the Family Court.
- Understated dower to "keep things simple" is a decision the bride's side pays for at the worst possible moment. The dower is the wife's financial floor in the marriage; set it deliberately.

## Column 17: conditions of the marriage

Column 17 asks whether the parties have agreed any special conditions. Almost every form has a line drawn through it — yet this column can lawfully carry real protections: the wife's right to continue education or employment, the residence she is entitled to (including a separate residence), that the husband will not contract a second marriage without her written consent, monthly maintenance amounts, or which city the couple will live in. Conditions not repugnant to the marriage's essence are enforceable contractual terms. This column is where a thoughtful family negotiates once, in goodwill, the matters that otherwise surface years later in hostile courtrooms.

## Column 18: the delegated right of divorce (talaq-e-tafweez)

The single most consequential column on the form. The husband may delegate to the wife the right to divorce herself. Where column 18 records the delegation, the wife can dissolve the marriage **without khula** — meaning without surrendering dower and without proving grounds — by exercising the delegated right and following the Union Council notice procedure.

Registrars habitually cross this column out without asking. Families should decide it consciously: the delegation costs the husband nothing while the marriage is sound, and gives the wife an exit of dignity if it is not. Column 19, its companion, asks whether the husband's own right of divorce has been curtailed in any agreed way.

## Columns 21–22: existing wife

These require disclosure of any existing marriage and the Arbitration Council's permission for a second one. A false declaration here is the foundation of later criminal liability.

## Alterations after the fact

Nikahnama tampering — inflating or deflating dower, filling column 18 later, "correcting" entries — appears constantly in litigation. The registered copy with the Union Council and the nikah registrar's record are the anchors; certified copies obtained early are the cheapest insurance against a doctored original appearing in court.

## The practical advice

Read the form before the event, in calm. Decide dower and its split deliberately. Use column 17 for what matters to the couple. Make a conscious decision on column 18. Obtain certified copies from the Union Council within days of registration and keep them separately. The nikahnama takes fifteen minutes to complete properly — and those fifteen minutes are the most valuable legal work most families will ever do without a lawyer.
