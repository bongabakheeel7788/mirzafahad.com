When a landowner dies, his land does not move by itself. Until an **inheritance mutation** (intiqal-e-wirasat) is entered and attested in the revenue record, the record still shows a dead man as owner — and nothing can be lawfully sold, partitioned or mortgaged on that land without trouble. This is the process in Punjab, and the traps within it.

## What a mutation is

A mutation is not a document of title; it is the revenue record's recognition of a change that has already occurred by law. Ownership passes to the heirs at the moment of death, in their fixed Islamic shares. The mutation records who those heirs are and in what shares, so the fard (record of rights) reflects reality. This distinction matters: a wrong mutation does not defeat a rightful heir's ownership, but it does force them into proceedings to correct it — sometimes decades later.

## The process, step by step

1. **Death certificate** from the Union Council where the death was registered.
2. **Heirship verification.** The backbone of the mutation is the list of legal heirs. NADRA's family records (Family Registration Certificate) now anchor this; the traditional route adds verification through the local Lambardar and revenue officials.
3. **Application at the Arazi Record Centre** (PLRA) of the tehsil where the land lies, with the death certificate, heirs' CNICs, and the deceased's ownership record (fard).
4. **Appearance and biometric verification of the heirs** at the centre.
5. **Attestation by the Revenue Officer.** The mutation is entered and then attested — in public proceedings where objections can be raised — after which the heirs appear in the record in their shares.

Fees are modest and fixed; there is no lawful "rate" beyond the schedule. The process, clean, takes weeks.

## The classic problems

**The omitted heir.** Daughters and sisters are the usual victims: the brothers process the mutation listing only themselves, or a daughter is recorded and then a "relinquishment" appears against her name. An omitted heir remains an owner in law, and can sue for her share whenever she discovers the omission — with superior courts repeatedly holding that fraud of this kind is not protected by the passage of time. If you are the omitted heir: obtain the mutation's copy and the fard, and file for correction/declaration promptly once you know.

**The manufactured relinquishment.** Thumb impressions of illiterate women on "formality papers" that turn out to surrender their inheritance. Courts scrutinise relinquishments by female heirs closely — especially without consideration, without independent advice, in favour of the very relatives processing the papers. Such documents are challengeable and regularly struck down.

**Mutation of a gift that never happened.** A deathbed "oral gift" mutation in favour of one son, entered after the father's death, defeating the inheritance of the rest. The law requires a valid gift to have been made in the donor's lifetime with possession delivered; the timing and evidence of these mutations rarely survive examination.

**Pending disputes.** Where heirship itself is contested — a second family, a disputed marriage — the Revenue Officer cannot decide title; the matter belongs in the civil court, and the mutation follows the decree.

## After the mutation

The mutation gives every heir a recorded share in every khewat — co-ownership, not a divided plot. Actually splitting the land is **partition**, a separate proceeding (revenue partition for agricultural land, or the civil court). Heirs intending to sell only their own share can do so, but a purchaser of an undivided share buys into co-ownership, which is why coordinated family decisions — partition first, then dealings — preserve value.

The single rule that prevents most inheritance litigation: process the mutation early, with every heir present and recorded. Delay is the fraudster's best friend.
