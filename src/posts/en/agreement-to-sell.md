Most property deals in Punjab begin with a bai'ana: earnest money changes hands, an agreement to sell is signed on stamp paper, and a date is fixed for the balance and the registry. Most property *litigation* begins there too. The agreement to sell is simultaneously the most important and the worst-drafted document in the market.

## What the agreement is

An agreement to sell does not transfer ownership — only the registered sale deed does. It creates **contractual rights**: the buyer's right to compel completion (specific performance) and the seller's right to the price or to forfeit earnest money on the buyer's default. Because everything later turns on its words, drafting is not a formality.

## The clauses that decide disputes

A serviceable agreement identifies the **parties** (CNICs) and confirms the seller's capacity — *all* co-owners must sign, a detail whose omission voids many deals against absent co-heirs; describes the **property precisely** (khewat/khasra or plot number, measurements, what fixtures are included); states the **total price, the earnest paid, and the exact schedule of the balance**; fixes the **completion date** and — critically — states whether **time is of the essence**; allocates **who bears taxes, dues and transfer costs**; contains the seller's **warranties** (ownership, no encumbrance, no litigation, no prior agreement); spells out **default consequences both ways** — seller's refusal (return of double earnest and/or buyer's right to specific performance) and buyer's default (forfeiture, and whether full or partial); and provides for **possession** (with the deed, or earlier against safeguards). Witnesses on both sides, every page signed, and payment through **banking channels** — cash paid without trace is the recurring tragedy of these cases.

## When the seller backs out

Rising prices are the usual motive. The buyer's remedy is a suit for **specific performance** under the Specific Relief Act: compelling execution of the sale deed on payment of the balance. What the buyer must prove has been distilled by the courts into a sharp point: continuous **readiness and willingness** to perform — meaning the money. A buyer who pleads willingness but cannot show he had, and has, the balance available fails; current practice expects the buyer to demonstrate capacity concretely, and courts routinely require the balance to be **deposited in court** at the suit's early stage. File promptly (limitation applies from the refusal/completion date), seek an injunction against onward sale the same day, and put your funds where the court can see them.

## When the buyer defaults

The seller's questions are two. **Forfeiture:** earnest money genuinely paid as earnest can be forfeited on the buyer's default — but courts police forfeiture of *large part-payments* as penalties, so the clause's wording and the payment's characterisation matter. **Moving on:** the seller should formally terminate — written notice through counsel, recording the default and forfeiture — before reselling; a silent resale while the old agreement dangles invites a specific-performance suit against the new transaction, with the new buyer joined.

## The traps

Oral extensions of the completion date (get every extension in writing, signed); part-payments continuing informally after the deadline, which courts read as the parties themselves treating time as *not* essential; agreements signed by one brother "on behalf of the family"; sellers signing while the property stands mortgaged; and photocopy agreements — the original, with its stamp paper serial, is the case.

## Practical rule

Spend one day and one modest drafting fee on the agreement, pay every rupee through the bank, and diarise the completion date with your lawyer a week in advance. The parties who do this either complete smoothly or litigate from strength; the parties who sign the property dealer's template in the office generally fund the district bar for years.
