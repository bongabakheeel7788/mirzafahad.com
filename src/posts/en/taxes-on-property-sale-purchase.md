Every property deal in Pakistan carries two negotiations: one over the price, and a quieter one over who absorbs the taxes. Buyers discover levies at the registry they never budgeted; sellers discover that the "small" withholding is only the advance instalment of a bigger gain tax. The rates move with every Finance Act, but the architecture is stable — and knowing the architecture is what prevents transfer-day surprises.

## The two value tables

Before any rate matters, understand what it is applied to. Pakistan runs **two official value tables** for property:

- **DC rates** (district collector valuation tables) — fixed per mauza/area by the provincial authorities; the base for **provincial** levies such as stamp duty.
- **FBR values** — the federal valuation tables notified for urban areas, generally set closer to (but still usually below) market; the base for **federal** withholding and gain taxes. Where FBR has notified no value for an area, the DC rate steps in.

Taxes are computed on the **higher of declared consideration and the applicable table value**. The old habit of writing a token price in the deed no longer saves federal tax where tables apply — and understating still creates the benami/unexplained-investment exposure this series has covered, because the money trail and the paper trail stop matching.

## What the buyer pays

- **Advance income tax under section 236K**, collected by the registering or transferring authority at transfer. The rate ladder depends on status: **filer**, the **late filer** category created by the Finance Act 2024, or **non-filer** — each step materially more expensive, with recent law moving toward outright restrictions on large purchases by non-filers. For a filer this tax is **adjustable**: it credits against the year's tax liability through the return.
- **Provincial charges**: stamp duty on the conveyance, registration fee, and local levies — the Punjab side of which (including e-Stamping) this series covers separately.

Budget both columns before committing, and confirm your ATL status **before** the transfer appointment, not at it.

## What the seller pays

- **Advance tax under section 236C**, collected at transfer, again status-graded, and adjustable against the seller's gain tax liability for filers.
- **Capital gains tax under section 37(1A)** of the Income Tax Ordinance 2001 on the profit. The regime split by the **Finance Act 2024**: for properties **acquired on or after 1 July 2024**, filers pay a flat rate on the gain regardless of holding period, while non-filers face higher, income-slab-linked taxation; for **earlier-acquired** properties, the older holding-period schedule (rates stepping down the longer you held, reaching zero after the statutory years) continues to govern. Which schedule applies to your plot therefore turns on one date in your file — the acquisition date — and the law in force in the year of sale.

The gain is **sale value minus cost**, so preserve the evidence of cost: the old deed or allotment, improvement expenses, transfer charges. Sellers who paid genuine millions in construction but kept no record pay tax on fictional profit.

## Exemptions and edge cases

The Ordinance has carried reliefs whose details shift year to year — the personal-residence relief, treatment of inherited property (inheritance itself is not a purchase; the heir's cost and holding reckon by reference to the inherited position, and gain tax arises on the heir's **sale**), and dependants-of-shaheed/serving-personnel exemptions in certain years. Two practical constants:

- **Gifts and inheritance transfers** are not sales, so 236C/K generally do not bite the way they do on sale — but the subsequent sale by the donee or heir is fully in the net.
- **Open-file and society transfers** without registered deeds do not escape the regime: FBR values and withholding reach notified schemes, and the society's transfer process collects the taxes.

## The checklist before transfer day

1. Verify **both parties' ATL status** and the current year's 236C/236K rates.
2. Pull the **FBR valuation table and DC rate** for the exact mauza/block.
3. Compute the seller's **CGT by acquisition date** under the schedule in force.
4. Agree **in writing** who bears which tax — the law assigns incidence, but parties routinely reallocate by contract, and silent deals breed transfer-day deadlock.
5. Keep every **CPR (tax payment receipt)** — they are your adjustment and refund evidence.

Because the rates and restriction rules genuinely change every budget, treat every number you have heard as a question to verify, not a fact. A brief consultation with the title documents, acquisition papers and both CNICs — a week before transfer, not the morning of — prices the entire transaction and prevents the registry-counter renegotiation that derails so many deals.
