Ask ten buyers in Faisalabad what completes a property purchase and half will say "the registry," the other half "the intiqal." Both halves are wrong, because the honest answer is: both, in the right order. The registered deed and the mutation do different legal jobs, and the space between them is where double sales, frozen bank accounts and decade-long suits are born.

## What the registry is

The "registry" is a **registered sale deed** — a conveyance executed before the sub-registrar under the **Registration Act 1908**, read with section 54 of the **Transfer of Property Act 1882**, which requires the sale of immovable property of any meaningful value to be made by registered instrument. Registration fixes the deed's date, identifies the parties through verification, preserves a copy in the state's record, and gives the world notice of the transfer.

Legally, this is the document that **transfers ownership**. When the deed is validly executed and registered, title passes from seller to buyer — whatever the revenue record says that afternoon.

## What the mutation is

The **mutation (intiqal)** is an entry in the revenue record under the **Land Revenue Act 1967**. When a transfer occurs — sale, gift, inheritance, mortgage — the revenue machinery records it so that the record of rights stays current and land revenue is collected from the right person. The revenue officer attests the mutation, ideally after notice to interested parties.

Here is the point our superior courts have repeated for decades: **a mutation does not create or extinguish title**. It is evidence of a transaction, made for fiscal purposes, and its evidentiary value depends on the transaction behind it being proved. A person whose claim rests on nothing but a mutation entry — no deed, no consideration proved, no witnesses to the underlying transaction — holds a famously weak position in court.

## Why you need both

Think of it as two registers that must agree:

- The **registered deed** gives you title and the document you will produce in any court contest.
- The **mutation** puts your name into the record every future actor consults — the patwari, the Arazi Record Centre, the bank assessing a loan, the sub-registrar in your onward sale, the tax authorities.

A buyer who stops at the registry owns land that the record still attributes to the seller. The seller's creditors may attach it; the seller's heirs may "inherit" it on paper; a dishonest seller may parade the clean fard before a second buyer. The second buyer's position will eventually fail against your registered deed, but "eventually" in civil litigation is measured in years. A buyer who stops at the mutation, conversely — in a transaction where a registered deed was required — may hold an entry with no valid transfer underneath it at all.

## The 2015 change every villager should know

Rural Punjab long ran on oral sales recorded by mutation — no deed, just the parties before the revenue officer. Punjab closed that door by amending the **Land Revenue Act in 2015**: sale mutations now require a registered deed. If someone proposes an old-style oral sale "like we have always done it," the law has moved on, and so should your transaction.

## The sequence that protects a buyer

1. **Before paying:** obtain a fresh fard, verify the seller's share, check encumbrances and pending mutations.
2. **Execute and register the sale deed** before the sub-registrar, with proper stamp duty and taxes paid — under-stamping has its own painful consequences.
3. **Apply for the mutation immediately** on the strength of the registered deed, through the Arazi Record Centre process.
4. **Collect the attested mutation copy and a fresh fard** showing your name. Only now is the file complete.
5. Keep deed, mutation and fard together permanently; your heirs will need the full chain.

Sellers have a mirror interest: a seller who signs a deed but lets the mutation drift stays on the revenue record for land he no longer owns, with everything that implies for tax notices and liability questions.

## When the two records conflict

Conflicts — a deed in one name, the mutation in another, or a mutation attested behind a co-sharer's back — are resolved in civil court through suits for declaration and cancellation, and the registered instrument with a proven transaction behind it generally prevails over a bare entry. But prevention costs a fraction of that suit. If your registry and record do not match, or you are mid-purchase and unsure what is missing, a brief consultation with the deed, fard and mutation copies on the table settles the strategy quickly.
