Maintenance cases are the bread and butter of Pakistan's Family Courts, and they are decided faster and more mechanically than most people expect. If you understand three things — who is entitled, how the amount is fixed, and how orders are enforced — you understand the case.

## Who is entitled

**Children.** A father must maintain his children: sons until majority (and beyond, while reasonably in education, per consistent case law), daughters until marriage. The duty exists regardless of custody, regardless of the mother's income, and regardless of the state of the parents' marriage. It includes food, clothing, housing, education and medical care.

**Wife.** A husband must maintain his wife during the marriage and during iddat after divorce. A wife who leaves the house without lawful cause weakens her claim — but "lawful cause" includes non-payment of maintenance itself, cruelty, or the husband's failure to provide a separate residence where she is entitled to one. A wife's own employment does not extinguish the husband's obligation, though it can affect quantum.

**Parents.** Less commonly litigated, but children of means are obliged to maintain indigent parents.

## How the amount is fixed

The Family Court looks at the payer's means and the claimants' needs. In practice, courts triangulate from: the father's declared income and — because declarations are routinely understated — his **lifestyle and assets**: the car he drives, the business he runs, property in his name, his travel. Courts openly draw inferences against a father who conceals income; a man who pleads poverty from behind the wheel of a new car fares poorly.

School fee receipts, utility bills and grocery estimates anchor the children's needs. Orders in district courts commonly run from a few thousand rupees per child per month at the lower end to substantial five-figure sums where means are proven. The decree ordinarily includes an **annual increase** (often ten percent or as the court fixes) so the order keeps pace without fresh litigation.

## Past maintenance

Maintenance can be claimed for the past, not just the future. Limitation restricts how far back the claim reaches, and courts assess it more conservatively — but a father who paid nothing for three years should expect a significant lump-sum liability, not a clean slate.

## Interim maintenance — the pressure point

The Family Courts Act requires the court to fix **interim maintenance** at an early stage, and here is the provision with teeth: if the defendant fails to pay interim maintenance, the court can strike off his defence and decree the suit. Non-payment during the case is therefore not a delay tactic; it is surrender.

## Enforcement

A maintenance decree is executed like any decree — attachment of salary or bank accounts, attachment and sale of property — and the Family Court can also treat non-payment in ways that end with the defaulter in civil prison. Where the father is employed, salary attachment through the employer is the most effective route. Where he is abroad, execution can proceed against his assets in Pakistan.

## Practical advice

For mothers and wives: file with documents — fee vouchers, rent agreement, medical bills, and every proof of the father's means you can lawfully obtain. Ask for interim maintenance at the first hearing.

For fathers: pay the interim order even while contesting quantum; contest with documents (actual income proof), not with denials; and remember that maintaining your children generously and voluntarily is also, in a custody or visitation dispute, the best evidence of the parent you claim to be.
