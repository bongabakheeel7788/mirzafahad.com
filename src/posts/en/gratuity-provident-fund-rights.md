The last salary is paid, the clearance form is signed, and then the figure that should have been the largest — years of gratuity and provident fund — quietly never arrives. End-of-service dues are the most commonly withheld money in Pakistani private employment, and also among the most clearly owed. The law here is older than most employers realise and more generous than most workers assume.

## The three components

**Gratuity** is a reward for service the law reads into covered employment. Under the **Industrial and Commercial Employment (Standing Orders) Ordinance 1968**, a workman whose service ends is entitled to **thirty days' wages for every completed year of service** — with a period of six months or more counting as a full year — calculated on the wages last drawn. It is the employer's unilateral debt; no fund, no deduction from the worker, no scheme membership is needed.

**Provident fund** is different: a contributory scheme the employer may establish, with matching deductions from wages accumulating in the worker's account. Where an employer maintains an approved provident fund to which it contributes at least the prescribed level, the Ordinance relieves it of separate gratuity — the classic **either/or**: you are entitled to gratuity **or** the employer's recognised provident fund, and an employer running neither simply owes gratuity.

**Leave encashment** is payment for earned annual leave standing unused at the end of service, in accordance with the Factories Act 1934 or the Shops and Establishments Ordinance 1969 regime applicable to the establishment, and the terms of employment.

## Who is covered

The Standing Orders Ordinance applies to industrial and commercial establishments above the statutory employee threshold, and protects **workmen** — the skilled, unskilled, technical and clerical workforce. Managerial staff fall back on their contracts; many well-drafted contracts and company policies grant gratuity or fund benefits anyway, and those promises are enforceable as contract. Government and statutory-body employees have their own rules entirely.

Two realities of the Faisalabad labour market deserve naming. First, **contractor-supplied and "temporary" workers** doing perennial work are routinely denied dues on the theory that they were never employees; courts pierce that arrangement where the work and control were really the principal employer's. Second, employers sometimes maintain a provident fund **on paper** while never actually depositing contributions — the worker is then entitled to what the fund should have held, and the deduction shown in salary slips is powerful evidence.

## Resignation, dismissal and death

- **Resignation does not forfeit gratuity.** The entitlement arises on resignation exactly as on termination or retirement. An employer's policy saying otherwise cannot defeat the statute for covered workmen.
- **Dismissal for misconduct** can affect gratuity only within narrow bounds — the classic case being misconduct causing financial loss, where the loss may be set off. Dismissal does not erase provident fund balances: the worker's own contributions and their growth are always his, and forfeiting the employer's share is only possible to the limited extent the fund's rules validly provide.
- **Death in service**: dues become payable to the legal heirs, and the Standing Orders treat death as entitling the family to gratuity. Heirs should claim with the NADRA succession documentation this series has covered elsewhere.

## Calculating before you negotiate

Before any settlement talk, compute your claim: last drawn monthly wages × years of completed service (gratuity), plus the provident fund ledger balance (ask for the fund statement — you are entitled to it), plus unused leave. "Wages" disputes are common — employers calculate on basic pay alone while workers claim gross; what counts depends on the statutory definition and how the wage was structured, and it materially changes the figure. Get the calculation checked rather than accepting the clearance sheet's arithmetic.

## Recovery when the employer will not pay

For workmen in Punjab, unpaid end-of-service dues go to the labour forums — a claim before the **Payment of Wages Authority** for wage-type dues within its jurisdiction, or the **Labour Court**, including through the grievance procedure of the Punjab Industrial Relations Act 2010. These forums are faster and cheaper than civil court, can award the dues with compensation, and their orders are executable. Act quickly: labour-side limitation periods are short, and a "full and final settlement" receipt signed under financial pressure will be the employer's first exhibit — sign nothing that recites satisfaction of dues you have not received.

Most dues cases are arithmetic plus one legal question: were you a workman, and was there a real provident fund? A brief consultation with your appointment letter, salary slips and any fund statement answers both and puts a precise figure on the demand before the first notice goes out.
