People research a phone purchase for a week and choose a lawyer in an afternoon — usually on a relative's recommendation, in a crisis, under time pressure. Since the choice shapes years of your life, here is a candid framework from inside the profession.

## Verify the basics

An advocate must be enrolled with the provincial Bar Council (Punjab Bar Council for this region) — licensed for the courts they'll appear in: lower courts, High Court, or Supreme Court enrolments are distinct. Verification is straightforward: enrolment details, the district bar's membership, a visible chamber. This filters out the courthouse's fringe of touts and fixers who impersonate counsel — if your first contact was a stranger who approached *you* near the gate, walk away.

## Match the lawyer to the matter

Law has specialisations exactly as medicine does. The advocate who excels in murder trials may be adrift in a banking suit; the property specialist may not know family-court rhythms. Ask directly: *"How much of your practice is cases like mine? What were your last few such cases and how did they proceed?"* Specific answers signal experience; grand generalities signal marketing. For most district-level matters you want someone who is **in those courtrooms weekly** — familiarity with the forum's practice is worth as much as brilliance.

## The first consultation tells you everything

Bring your documents and watch how they work: a good lawyer **reads before opining**, asks factual questions, and then explains — in your language — the realistic strengths, weaknesses, options, timeline and cost. Beware the two failure modes: the **guaranteer** ("hundred percent we win, don't worry") — no honest lawyer guarantees outcomes, and this pitch precedes most fee disappointments; and the **fog machine**, who keeps everything vague so you can never evaluate progress. You are hiring judgment, and the consultation is your sample of it.

## Fees: get the structure straight

Understand what the quoted fee covers: the whole case through judgment, or per stage (institution, evidence, arguments), or per appearance? What's excluded — court fees, copying, clerk expenses, appeals, execution? Is any part contingent on milestones? **Get it in writing** — a simple engagement note or even a WhatsApp summary both sides confirm — and pay traceably with receipts. Cheap is not the goal; *defined* is. The commonest client grievance in Pakistan is not fee size but fee surprise — and the commonest lawyer grievance is the client who stops paying mid-case, so clarity protects the relationship both ways.

## The working relationship

Agree communication expectations upfront: updates after every hearing (a message with what happened and the next date is reasonable to expect), access for questions, and honesty about delays. Your obligations mirror theirs: complete truth (cases die from facts clients hid from their own counsel), documents on time, presence when required, and patience with the system's pace — your lawyer does not control adjournments, strikes or the judge's roster.

## Changing counsel

You are entitled to change lawyers; cases move on a fresh power of attorney with the file. Do it for competence or communication failures — not because an opponent's tout whispered doubts, a standard destabilisation tactic. And when you leave, settle accounts cleanly; the bar is small and your file's next custodian will hear how the last engagement ended.

## The summary test

Choose the advocate who reads your papers before promising, names weaknesses you didn't want to hear, writes the fee down, and answers messages. Those four behaviours at the start predict the next three years better than any signboard.
