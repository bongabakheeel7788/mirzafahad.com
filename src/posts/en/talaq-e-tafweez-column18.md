At most nikah ceremonies in Punjab, columns 18 and 19 of the nikahnama are crossed out with two quick strokes of the registrar's pen before anyone at the table has read them. Column 18 asks a single question: has the husband delegated the right of divorce to the wife? For the families who understand it, that one column is the difference between a wife who can exit a broken marriage with her dower intact and one who must litigate khula for a year and surrender her mehr for her freedom.

## What delegation means

Under Muslim law the right of talaq belongs to the husband, but he may delegate it — *tafweez* — to his wife, either at the time of nikah or later, unconditionally or on conditions (for instance, exercisable if he contracts a second marriage or fails to maintain her). Delegation does not transfer the right away from him; it duplicates it. He keeps his talaq; she gains her own.

The nikahnama, prescribed under the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, builds the option into the standard form: **column 18** asks whether the husband has delegated the power of divorce to the wife, and if so, under what conditions. An entry of "yes" — ideally with conditions spelled out or expressly stated as unconditional — is the wife's standing authority for the rest of the marriage.

## How a wife exercises it

If column 18 is filled in her favour, a wife ends the marriage without filing any suit:

1. **Pronounce the divorce** upon herself under the delegated authority — in writing is best, referencing column 18 of the registered nikahnama.
2. **Send written notice** to the chairman of the Union Council (the one with jurisdiction per the rules), with a copy to the husband, exactly as section 7 of the MFLO requires for a husband's talaq. The section applies to delegated divorce through section 8.
3. **The arbitration council** stage follows: the chairman constitutes it and attempts reconciliation.
4. **After ninety days** from delivery of notice, if reconciliation has not succeeded, the divorce becomes effective and the Union Council issues the certificate — the document NADRA and everyone else will later demand.

Skipping the notice is the classic mistake, with the same consequences as in an ordinary talaq: doubt over the effective date, complications over remarriage, and iddat-period disputes.

## Why it beats khula

A wife without column 18 who wants out of the marriage must file for **khula** in the family court. Khula is now granted readily — the Punjab amendments let the court decree it at the pre-trial stage when reconciliation fails — but it ordinarily costs the wife her dower, which the court directs her to return or forgo as consideration. Talaq-e-tafweez is legally a **talaq**: the wife's mehr, prompt and deferred, survives, along with her maintenance for iddat. For a woman whose mehr is substantial — gold, land, a house recorded in the nikahnama — the difference is enormous.

## Negotiating it at the time of nikah

- Insist that columns 18 and 19 are **read aloud and discussed**, not pre-crossed by the nikah registrar out of habit.
- An unconditional delegation gives maximum protection; a conditional one (second marriage, non-maintenance, cruelty) is a common middle ground that most families accept.
- Ensure the entry is in the registered copy, not just the family's copy — certified copies from the Union Council record are what courts act on.
- A delegation can also be made **after** marriage by a written instrument; couples who crossed out column 18 at nikah can still fix it.

## If the husband disputes the exercise

Husbands sometimes contest that the delegation existed or that conditions were met. The registered nikahnama is a public record, and a certified copy ordinarily settles the existence question. Conditional delegations invite factual disputes — whether maintenance was really withheld — which may end up before the family court in collateral proceedings such as the wife's suit for dower or maintenance. Clean drafting in column 18 at the outset prevents most of this.

Whether you are drafting a nikahnama for a daughter, checking your own column 18, or ready to exercise a delegation, the documents decide everything: the registered nikahnama, the notice, and the Union Council record. A brief consultation with those papers usually settles the route — and the ninety-day clock — in a single sitting.
