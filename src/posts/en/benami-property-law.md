Putting property in another person's name is one of the oldest habits in Pakistani money — the plot in a brother's name, the house in a trusted servant's name, the factory shares parked with a cousin. Since the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act 2017** came into force with its rules, that habit carries risks most families have not absorbed: confiscation without compensation, and prison for both sides of the arrangement.

## What "benami" means in law

A benami transaction is one where property is held by one person (the **benamidar** — the name-lender) but the consideration was paid by another (the **beneficial owner**), for that other's present or future benefit. The name on the registry is a mask; the real owner stands behind it. The 2017 Act also catches transactions in fictitious names, and those where the recorded owner denies knowledge of or cannot account for the property.

The classic motives are concealment: hiding assets from tax authorities, from creditors, from a spouse in divorce, from co-heirs, or placing untaxed money beyond inquiry. The law now treats the arrangement itself as the offence, whatever the motive.

## What the 2017 Act changed

Before 2017, benami was mainly a civil-court question — who really owns this plot? The Act federalised and criminalised it:

- **Prohibition:** entering into a benami transaction is prohibited outright.
- **Confiscation:** property held benami is liable to **confiscation by the federal government**, through adjudicating authorities established under the Act, with appeals to a tribunal and onward to the High Court. The beneficial owner loses the asset; the benamidar cannot lawfully retransfer it to him.
- **Prosecution:** both benamidar and beneficial owner — and those who abet the arrangement — face rigorous imprisonment and fines on conviction.
- **No recovery suit:** the beneficial owner cannot sue the benamidar to recover the property; the law deliberately leaves him empty-handed if his name-lender turns dishonest.

Enforcement runs through FBR-side machinery, and activity has come in waves — but the statute sits on the books, and any dispute, audit or hostile relative can bring a dormant arrangement into its light.

## The exceptions that protect ordinary families

The Act does not criminalise normal family life. Key carve-outs:

- Property purchased in the name of a **spouse or child**, from the buyer's **known resources** — the salaried father buying a house in his wife's name from declared income is outside the Act.
- Property held by a person in a **fiduciary capacity** — a trustee, executor, partner, or director holding for the firm or beneficiary.
- Joint purchases with **brothers, sisters or lineal ascendants/descendants** from known resources, where the property is held jointly.

The recurring phrase is *known resources*. The exception shields the transaction only when the money trail is clean and declared; a purchase in the wife's name from undeclared cash fails the very condition that makes it lawful.

## Benami fights inside families

Most benami litigation is not FBR versus tycoon; it is brother versus brother after the father dies. One heir says the land in his name was bought by him; the others say the father paid and parked it. Courts resolve these on settled factors:

1. **Source of consideration** — who actually paid? This is the heaviest factor.
2. **Possession and enjoyment** — who held, used, rented, and took the income?
3. **Custody of title documents** — the real owner usually keeps the registry and fard.
4. **Motive** — was there an intelligible reason to use another's name?
5. **Relationship and conduct** of the parties before and after.

The burden lies squarely on whoever alleges benami, and suspicion or close kinship alone never discharges it. Evidence assembled at the time — bank instruments for the price, receipts, correspondence — decides these cases decades later.

## Practical guidance

- Buying for a spouse or child: do it from declared funds, pay through banking channels, and keep the trail.
- Holding property for someone else "temporarily": understand you may be a benamidar in law, exposed to prosecution and unable to lawfully hand it back if confiscation proceedings start.
- Suspecting a benami against your inheritance: start collecting the payment-trail evidence now, not after the suit is filed.

Whether a given arrangement is an innocent family purchase, a fiduciary holding, or a prohibited benami turns entirely on its documents and money trail. A brief consultation with the registry, the payment record and the family facts on the table settles which side of the line you are on — and how to fix it if you are on the wrong one.
