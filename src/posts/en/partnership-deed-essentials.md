Most Pakistani partnerships are formed in an hour of goodwill and dissolved in years of litigation. The instrument that determines which fate awaits yours is the partnership deed — and the difference between a serviceable deed and a bazaar template is about ten clauses.

## First, the legal frame

The Partnership Act, 1932 governs. Partners are **jointly and severally liable** for the firm's obligations — each partner's personal assets stand behind the whole business, and each partner's acts within the business's ordinary course bind all. Registration with the **Registrar of Firms** is not mandatory — but an unregistered firm suffers a crippling disability: it **cannot sue** its own partners or third parties to enforce contractual rights. Every serious firm should register; the cost is trivial against the disability.

## The ten clauses

**1. Capital and contribution.** Who contributes what — cash, property, stock, skill — valued how, and whether contributions earn any return before profit-sharing. Property brought in should be formally transferred to the firm; "my shop, our business" ambiguity is the seed of the worst disputes.

**2. Profit and loss ratios.** Stated expressly — including whether loss-sharing differs from profit-sharing, and how drawings against profit are limited and recorded.

**3. Roles and authority limits.** Who manages what, and — decisive in practice — what **no partner may do alone**: borrow beyond a stated amount, sign cheques above a threshold (dual signatures work), sell firm assets, give guarantees, hire family members. Authority limits convert betrayal into provable breach.

**4. Banking and books.** The firm's accounts, who operates them, mandatory routing of all revenue through firm accounts, monthly accounts to every partner, and each partner's **right to inspect books** — with an annual reconciliation partners must sign.

**5. Remuneration vs profit.** Whether working partners draw a salary before profit split — the classic sleeping-partner vs working-partner friction, resolved in one sentence if written.

**6. Admission of new partners and outsiders.** Unanimity or defined majority; and a bar on partners' relatives acquiring roles or authority without consent.

**7. Exit: retirement and buyout.** How a partner leaves — notice, valuation method for his share (book value? independent valuation? formula?), payment schedule, and liability for pre-exit obligations, plus **public notice** of retirement, without which the retired partner keeps incurring liability to third parties who dealt on the old faith.

**8. Death of a partner.** By default, death can dissolve the firm — chaos for a running business. Provide expressly: the firm continues, the deceased's share is valued and paid to **legal heirs** on a schedule, and whether heirs may opt in as partners. This clause alone prevents the standard tragedy of a widow litigating against her husband's partners for a decade.

**9. Non-compete and firm property.** Partners not to divert firm business, clients or staff during the partnership and for a stated period after exit; firm name, licences, goodwill and tenancy rights belong to the firm, not to whoever's name they stand in.

**10. Dispute resolution and dissolution.** An escalation path — accounts review, mediation/arbitration under the Arbitration law — before court; and the dissolution mechanics: winding up, who collects receivables, how stock and assets are divided or auctioned between partners.

## The housekeeping that keeps deeds alive

Execute on proper stamp paper, all partners signing with witnesses; register the firm and obtain the NTN in the firm's name; and — most neglected — **amend the deed in writing** when reality changes (new ratios, a partner's exit, capital injections). Courts confront firms whose deed describes a business that stopped existing years earlier; the paper's silence is then filled by testimony, and testimony is where partnerships go to die.

A final honesty: the deed cannot make a bad partner good. It makes a bad partner *accountable* — and gives the good partner the exit, the valuation and the remedies that turn a betrayal into a solvable legal problem rather than a life's loss.
