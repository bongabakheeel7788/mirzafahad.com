Every trading business in Pakistan carries a tail of receivables that has quietly become bad debt — customers who "will pay after Eid", buyers disputing quality after consuming the goods, dealers rolling old balances into new orders. Collection is a discipline with an escalation ladder; businesses that climb it deliberately recover multiples of those that oscillate between pleading and rage.

## Rung zero: the paperwork you build before trouble

Recovery is decided at sale time. Deliver against **signed delivery challans**; invoice promptly, with payment terms stated; reconcile accounts periodically and get the customer's **signed or emailed balance confirmation** — the single most powerful document in receivable litigation, since it collapses years of transactions into one admitted figure; and take **post-dated or security cheques** for credit lines. Businesses selling on credit without these are lending unsecured, informally, at scale.

## Rung one: structured chasing

Systematise reminders: statement of account, then a written demand from management, each with a date and mode of record (email, courier with receipt). Two disciplines matter: keep supplying **only against fresh payment** once a balance ages — the "one more order and I'll clear everything" pattern is how debts double; and log every promise (date, amount, who made it). This file later reads to a judge as a patient creditor and an evasive debtor — exactly the impression that wins interim orders.

## Rung two: the legal notice

A counsel's **demand notice** — stating the account, the admitted balance, the demand and a deadline — moves a startling share of stuck receivables, because it signals that the cost-benefit has changed. It also crystallises the record: replies often admit the balance while pleading time or raising (newly-invented) quality disputes, and both admissions and late-invented defences serve you in court.

## Rung three: choosing the right suit

**With a dishonoured cheque or written acknowledgment:** the summary suit under **Order XXXVII CPC** — the defendant must obtain leave to defend, decrees come quickest, and limitation runs from the instrument. This is the flagship commercial recovery route; protect it by banking cheques promptly and keeping dishonour memos.

**On open account:** an ordinary **suit for recovery** on the ledger — supported by challans, invoices, confirmations and the dealings' course. Slower, but sound books win these; the defence's usual "goods were defective" collapses against years of unprotested invoices and continued orders.

**The criminal lever:** where cheques bounced, **section 489-F** adds pressure (see our dedicated article) — used honestly as leverage toward settlement, since conviction punishes but does not repay. Pure unpaid invoices without cheques are civil matters; forcing them into FIRs backfires.

Interim tools matter in commercial suits: **attachment before judgment** where the debtor is stripping assets, and injunctions against specific dissipation — ask for them with evidence of flight risk, not as ritual.

## Rung four: settlement — engineered, not hoped for

Most matters end in settlement; the craft is structuring it so default restarts nothing: a written settlement recording the **full admitted debt**, instalments with dates, **post-dated cheques for each instalment** (every future default becomes fresh 489-F leverage), a clause reviving the full balance on any default, and withdrawal of litigation only on completion — not on signing.

## Portfolio honesty

Age your receivables monthly; act at 90 days, not at "when relations spoil" — limitation for these claims is short, acknowledgments extend it, and the debtor's other creditors are climbing their ladders too. Assets go to the creditor who arrives; in collection, courtesy and speed are not opposites — the most effective businesses are unfailingly polite and always one rung higher than the debtor expected.
