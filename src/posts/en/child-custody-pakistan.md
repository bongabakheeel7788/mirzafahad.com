No principle is repeated more often in Pakistani family courtrooms than this one: in custody, the **welfare of the minor** is the paramount consideration. Every other rule — the mother's preferential custody of young children, the father's position as natural guardian — bends to it. Understanding how courts apply that principle is the key to any custody case.

## The legal framework

Custody (hizanat) is decided by the Guardian Court under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, read with principles of Muslim personal law. Two distinct concepts matter: **custody** — with whom the child lives day to day — and **guardianship** of the child's person and property, which concerns legal authority and remains, presumptively, with the father. A mother with custody does not need guardianship to raise the child; a father with guardianship does not automatically get custody.

## The mother's custody — and its limits

Classical principle gives the mother preferential custody of a son until roughly seven years and of a daughter until puberty. But Pakistani courts treat these ages as guidelines, not cut-offs: custody does not transfer automatically at seven, and courts routinely continue a child with the mother beyond the classical ages where welfare demands it — which, in practice, it often does, particularly where the child has been settled with her for years.

Circumstances said to disqualify a mother — most commonly her **remarriage** to a stranger to the child — are likewise not automatic. Remarriage is a factor courts weigh, not a rule that snatches custody; judgments exist both ways, always turning on the child's actual welfare and attachment.

## What "welfare" means in evidence

Courts look at concrete things: who has actually been raising the child; schooling and its continuity; the child's attachment and, for an older child, his or her own preference (judges frequently speak to the child in chambers); each side's home environment and character; financial capacity — which matters but does not outweigh care, since the father's duty to maintain the child exists regardless of custody; and any history of violence or neglect. A parent who abducted the child from settled custody starts on the back foot: courts strongly disapprove of self-help.

## Visitation: the certain outcome

Whichever way custody goes, the other parent will almost always receive a **visitation schedule** — typically fortnightly or monthly meetings, expanding to school holidays and Eid days as trust builds. Refusing to comply with a visitation order is contempt and, over time, a ground to reconsider custody itself. The wisest litigants treat visitation as the child's right rather than the opponent's concession.

## Interim orders and abduction

Custody cases take time, so the interim order — who keeps the child during the case — is often the real battle. Courts favour the status quo unless the child is at risk. Where a child is removed by force or stealth, the immediate remedy is a petition for restoration under the Act or, in urgent cases, habeas corpus before the High Court, which can bring the child before the court within days.

## Maintenance is separate

The father's obligation to maintain his children — school fees included — continues in full whether or not the mother has custody, and whether or not he is permitted the visitation he wants. Linking maintenance to custody demands ("I'll pay when I get the child") impresses no court.

## Practical advice

Keep the child's routine stable and documented — school records, medical records, photographs of daily life are quiet, powerful evidence. Never coach the child; judges detect it and it backfires. Comply with every court date and every visitation order to the letter. And on both sides: the parent who demonstrates that the child's life is calmer with them, rather than the parent who proves the other is wicked, usually leaves with the order they wanted.
