Most first-time filers are pushed into it by a transaction — a plot transfer taxed at non-filer rates, a car, a bank's questions — rather than by civic enthusiasm. However you arrive, the first return is the one that matters most: it sets the baseline every later year reconciles against. Done thoughtfully, it is a few evenings of work; done carelessly, it manufactures problems that surface years later as FBR notices.

## Who must file

Section **114 of the Income Tax Ordinance 2001** lists the persons obliged to file. The practical headline categories for individuals:

- **Taxable income above the basic threshold** for the year (the threshold and slabs are set by each Finance Act — check the current ones).
- **Every NTN/registration holder** — once registered, you file, income or not.
- **Property and vehicle markers**: ownership of immovable property of the sizes the section specifies (urban property of the stated area, flats, and similar markers), or a motor vehicle above the stated engine capacity.
- **Commercial and industrial electricity consumers** above the stated annual bill.
- **Professionals and association members** (chambers, bar associations, medical bodies and the like) under the listed clauses.
- **Foreign travel, foreign assets and other triggers** added over the years.

Note the design: the net is cast by **assets and lifestyle**, not just income. A retired person with no taxable income but an urban house of the specified size is still a required filer — the return simply shows the position.

## Getting onto IRIS

**IRIS** is FBR's online system; everything happens there. For an individual, the **CNIC number is the NTN**. Registration needs a mobile number registered against your own CNIC and an email address; the system verifies both. Salaried and business individuals use the same portal, choosing the return form the system serves for their profile. Overseas Pakistanis register and file the same way, with residence status determining what is taxable here.

Guard the credentials like a bank login. "Agents" who register you on their own mobile numbers and emails create a recurring disaster: years later the taxpayer cannot access their own account, reset codes go to a stranger, and undoing it costs real effort.

## What goes in the return

The return aggregates your year (1 July to 30 June):

- **Salary** — from the employer's certificate; tax withheld under section 149 appears as credit. Many salaried filers end at or near zero additional tax.
- **Business income** — for shopkeepers and traders, honest turnover and expense figures; small traders should ask about the simplified/fixed regimes in force for their year.
- **Property income** — rent is taxable under its own head with its own deductions and rates; one rented shop makes you a property-income taxpayer.
- **Profit on debt, dividends, prizes** — mostly already withheld at source; the return claims the credits.
- **Withholding credits generally** — mobile phone tax, vehicle token withholding, banking withholding, the 236-series property taxes: collect the certificates and CPRs, because these credits are the difference between owing and refund.

## The wealth statement: where returns are won and lost

Resident individuals must file the **section 116 wealth statement**: every asset at cost (plots, houses, vehicles, gold, bank balances, cash, business capital), every liability, and the year's personal expenses — plus the **reconciliation**: last year's wealth, plus this year's declared income and other inflows, minus expenses, must equal this year's wealth.

For a first-time filer the strategic question is the **opening position**: declare your existing assets honestly and completely at the start. Assets left out cannot be comfortably introduced later — a plot that suddenly appears in year three invites a notice asking what income bought it. Inherited and gifted assets have their own proper treatment and documentation (gift deeds, banking channels for cash gifts); record them correctly rather than inventing purchase stories.

## Deadlines and the cost of missing them

The individual deadline is normally **30 September** after the tax-year close, with extensions announced some years. Missing it risks **penalty under section 182**, drops you from the next ATL until you file with the **182A surcharge**, and — since the Finance Act 2024 — can place you in the costlier **late-filer** rate category for property transactions. Filing a nil or low-income return on time is cheap; restoring your position afterwards never is.

A first return built on honest numbers — CNIC, bank statements, salary certificate, property papers and a candid asset list — takes one sitting to organise. A brief consultation with those documents settles your filing obligation, your opening wealth statement and the credits you are already owed, before the deadline turns a simple task into a penalised one.
