The registered envelope with an advocate's letterhead triggers either panic or contempt — and both responses are errors. A legal notice is neither a court order nor a bluff to bin: it is the documented opening move of a dispute, and what you do in its deadline window shapes everything that follows.

## What a notice is, and why it exists

A legal notice is a formal communication, usually through counsel, stating a grievance, a demand and a deadline, with litigation the stated consequence. It exists for three reasons: some claims **require** it by statute before filing (consumer complaints' 15-day notice, defamation's notice stage, notices to government entities before suit, and others scattered through the statutes); it creates **evidence** — of demand, of the recipient's knowledge, of the positions each side took when memories were fresh; and it opens the **settlement window** that resolves a large share of disputes without a courtroom, as this series has noted from invoices to defamation.

Authenticity check, since fake "notices" circulate as pressure tactics: a genuine notice identifies the advocate, the client, the facts and demand, and arrives by provable means. A WhatsApp image of a smudged letterhead demanding cash to a personal account deserves the fraud treatment, not a reply.

## Reading it properly

With counsel, parse four things. **The claim's core**: what facts are alleged and what documents they imply the sender holds. **The demand**: money, retraction, possession, performance — and whether it is divisible (part admitted, part disputed). **The deadline and its nature**: contractual courtesy, or statutory window with consequences (defamation's regime, the consumer act's 15 days). **The forum threatened**: civil suit, criminal complaint, tribunal — which tells you the cost curve the sender contemplates.

## The reply

A proper reply, on your counsel's letterhead within the deadline, does four jobs: **corrects the record** — denying what is false specifically, not with a blanket "all contents denied" that reads as evasion; **asserts your case** — the facts and documents that answer the claim, stated firmly but without the rhetoric that reads badly in court exhibits later; **protects your position** — no unintended admissions, no waivers, reservation of rights and counterclaims (the notice you received may deserve one you send); and **opens or closes doors deliberately** — a without-prejudice settlement overture where resolution serves you, or a clear signal of contest where it does not.

What a reply must never do: admit facts casually ("the amount was indeed outstanding but…"), make threats that themselves create liability, or promise what you will not perform — every line may be Exhibit B.

## Silence, and when it is chosen

Ignoring a notice is occasionally a considered strategy — where any reply dignifies a shakedown, or engagement is premature — but it should be counsel's decision, made knowing the specific notice's statutory weight, never the default of avoidance. For the notices this series has covered — consumer, defamation, banking, tenancy — the windows are real and the costs of sleeping through them documented in each article.

The meta-lesson runs both directions: when *you* are aggrieved, the notice is your cheapest legal instrument — a few thousand rupees of drafting that resolves disputes, builds your record, and satisfies the statutes that demand it. The envelope works for whoever sends it properly.
