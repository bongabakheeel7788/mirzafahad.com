Reputation has always been actionable in Pakistan, but for decades defamation law was toothless in practice — suits crawled for years and awards were rare. That has changed, particularly in Punjab, and understanding the current architecture matters both for people whose names have been attacked and for those accused of attacking.

## The three legal tracks

**Civil defamation.** The Defamation Ordinance, 2002 created a statutory civil action: a person defamed by a false statement — spoken (slander) or written/broadcast (libel) — may sue for damages. In Punjab, this framework has been replaced by the **Punjab Defamation Act, 2024**, which established dedicated tribunals and a considerably sharper procedure (covered in detail in our companion article).

**Criminal defamation.** Sections 499–502 of the Pakistan Penal Code criminalise defamation, punishable with imprisonment or fine, prosecuted on complaint rather than FIR in the ordinary course. The section carries a long list of exceptions — truth for public good, good-faith opinions on public conduct, court reporting — which are the first checkpoint of any such complaint.

**PECA for online content.** Where the defamatory material is electronic, the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act adds its own offences relating to false information harming reputation and to offences against dignity — the route through the cybercrime machinery.

A claimant can realistically choose among these tracks, and the choice is strategic: civil for compensation and retraction, criminal or PECA for deterrent pressure — with courts increasingly wary of purely tactical criminal complaints.

## What a claimant must establish

The core elements are stable across tracks: a **statement** was made; it was **published** — communicated to at least one person other than the claimant (a WhatsApp group counts; a private message to the claimant alone does not); it **identified** the claimant expressly or by clear implication; and it was **defamatory** — tending to lower the claimant in the estimation of right-thinking members of society. Falsity is presumed in the claimant's favour once a statement is shown defamatory; proving truth is the defendant's burden.

Evidence discipline decides these cases: preserve the publication (notarised printouts, screenshots with URLs and dates, witnesses who saw it), prove authorship (account ownership, admissions, forwarding trails), and document the harm — lost contracts, social consequences, medical impact.

## The notice stage

Both the old Ordinance and the new Punjab Act build in a demand stage: the aggrieved person serves a **legal notice** seeking retraction and apology within the statutory window before or alongside proceedings. This stage settles a large share of matters — an unqualified public apology and retraction is often the claimant's genuine objective, and defendants who apologise early close cases that would otherwise cost them years and money. Never ignore a defamation notice; a timely, carefully drafted response — apology, justification, or clarification — shapes everything after.

## Realistic outcomes

Successful civil claims end in damages, mandatory apologies and orders restraining republication; the Punjab tribunals have added speed and presumptive damages that have made claims meaningfully more dangerous to ignore. Criminal complaints end more often in compromise than conviction. And a substantial share of all defamation litigation ends in negotiated retraction — which is why, on both sides, the early notice-and-response window is where the case is usually won.

If you are defamed: preserve first, notice second, choose the track third. If you are accused: stop republishing, take the notice to counsel the day it arrives, and treat apology not as defeat but as a strategic option with real value.
