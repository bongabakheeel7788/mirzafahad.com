Franchising and distribution are how ambitious Pakistani entrepreneurs buy speed: a proven brand, a supply chain, a format. The contracts behind them, however, are drafted by the brand, for the brand — and the investor who signs without adjustment discovers the imbalance only at renewal or termination, with a fitted-out premises and no brand to run in it. Here is the checklist for the other side of the table.

## Before the contract: diligence on the brand

Verify what you are actually buying: does the franchisor **own the trademark in Pakistan** (an IPO registration in the right classes, in the franchisor's name — not a pending application, not a cousin's name)? For international brands, is the local "master franchisee" genuinely authorised, and what happens to sub-franchisees if the master's rights end? Speak to existing franchisees — especially any who exited. And test the numbers independently; projections in franchise brochures are marketing, not warranty, and the contract will say exactly that.

## The money clauses

Map every payment: upfront franchise fee (what does it actually buy, and is any part refundable if approvals or site selection fail?); **royalties** (on gross or net? gross percentages punish thin-margin months); marketing-fund contributions (who controls the fund, and does it have accounting?); mandatory purchases — the quiet profit centre: where you must buy stock, packaging or equipment from the franchisor or nominated suppliers, is pricing controlled by any benchmark, or open-ended? and **security deposits** and bank guarantees, with their return conditions written.

## Territory and channel

Exclusivity is only as good as its drafting: define the territory precisely; state whether the franchisor may open its own outlets, grant others, or — the modern loophole — **sell online/through delivery apps into your territory** without compensation. Distribution agreements need the same channel honesty: institutional sales, e-commerce and direct company sales carve the value out of "exclusive" territories unless addressed.

## Operations, supply and fairness in performance

Targets should be realistic and consequences graduated — warning and cure periods, not instant termination on one missed quarter. Supply obligations must bind the franchisor too: service levels, lead times, and remedies **for you** when supply fails — a distributor punished for targets while starved of stock is the standard grievance. Price changes, credit terms and return/expiry policies for stock belong in writing, not in the relationship manager's discretion.

## Termination: read it as if it has happened

This is the clause to negotiate hardest. Check: term and **renewal** (a right to renew on stated conditions beats "mutual agreement", which means the brand's discretion at the moment your location has proven itself); termination grounds and cure periods both ways; and the **exit mathematics** — what is bought back (stock, equipment) at what value, who owns the location and its licences, how customer data and phone lines are handled, and the scope of **post-termination non-compete** (a reasonable restraint may stand; an attempt to sterilise your premises and capital indefinitely deserves resistance at drafting). Local reality check: your fit-out, deposits and market development are sunk with the brand — every clause above is really about who captures that sunk value at the end.

## Disputes and law

Foreign-brand contracts routinely impose foreign law and arbitration abroad — enforceable in principle under Pakistan's arbitration-convention framework, but expensive to actually use. Negotiate for arbitration seated in Pakistan or at minimum a carve-out for urgent local injunctive relief; ensure the Pakistani entity you contract with has assets worth suing.

## The negotiating posture

Franchise contracts are more negotiable than their "standard form" presentation suggests — before signing, never after. A day of counsel's markup on fees, territory, supply remedies and exit terms is the cheapest insurance on what is usually a family's largest single investment. The brand sells you its success story; the contract decides whether you ever get to keep your own.
