Punjab rewrote its defamation law in 2024, and the rewrite was not cosmetic. The Punjab Defamation Act, 2024 repealed the Defamation Ordinance's application in the province and built a new machine — one designed, its text says plainly, to make remedies for reputational harm fast and financially meaningful. Whatever one's view of the policy debate around it, anyone who posts, publishes or broadcasts about others in Punjab now operates under its rules.

## The structure

The Act covers defamation through **any medium** — print, broadcast, and expressly the online world of posts, videos and shares. Claims go not to the ordinary civil court but to **defamation tribunals** constituted for the purpose, headed by senior judicial figures, with appeals channelled upward on a tight leash. The Act sets **deadlines for the tribunal's process** — the design goal being decision in months, not the decade of the old regime.

## The provisions that changed behaviour

**Damages without proof of loss.** The Act's most consequential feature: general damages follow a successful claim without the claimant having to prove actual financial loss — with a substantial statutory floor, and higher exposure where the defamed person holds constitutional office or the publication is broadcast widely. The old defence-by-attrition — betting that the claimant could never prove quantified loss — is gone.

**The preliminary decree.** Where the tribunal, on the claim's early examination, finds the defendant has no plausible defence engaged, it can pass a preliminary decree — front-loading consequences that previously arrived only after full trial. Defendants can no longer treat the early stages as cost-free delay.

**Retraction and apology mechanics.** The Act formalises the offer-of-apology route with real procedural value for a defendant who moves quickly and sincerely — a genuine, prominent retraction remains the cheapest exit the law offers.

**Security and costs discipline.** The framework discourages both frivolous claims and dilatory defences with cost consequences — including for claims found to be brought to harass.

## Who is actually being sued under it

The tribunals' early docket is dominated by social-media matters: viral accusations against businesses, personal allegations in community and family disputes that migrated to Facebook, and political and journalistic content — the last driving the continuing public debate, and constitutional challenges, around the Act. For the ordinary user the lesson is less about press freedom and more immediate: **a shared or forwarded false accusation is a publication**, and the Act does not care that "everyone was posting it".

## Practical counsel

For potential claimants: the Act rewards preparation — preserve the publication chain, serve the statutory notice properly, and plead reach and persistence, which drive quantum. For anyone accused: the response window is now genuinely decisive; silence or bravado in the first weeks can walk you into a preliminary decree, while an early lawyer-drafted response — asserting truth or privilege where real, or negotiating retraction where not — keeps every option alive. And for businesses and pages that publish about others routinely: adopt a takedown-and-correction practice, because the cheapest defamation case is the one resolved before the notice period expires.

The constitutional challenges to parts of the Act continue, and its jurisprudence is young — this article will age. What will not age is the direction of travel: in Punjab, reputational harm has become expensive to inflict.
