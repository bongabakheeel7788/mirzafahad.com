A call from the police station — "aap ko IO ne bulaya hai" — triggers a fear that mostly comes from not knowing the rules. Investigation is a legal process with defined powers and defined limits. Knowing both converts panic into procedure.

## The call-up itself

Police may summon persons acquainted with a case's facts — witnesses and accused alike — typically by **notice under section 160 Cr.P.C.**. Attendance in response to a lawful notice is generally the right course; ignoring notices builds a record of evasion that surfaces at bail. But a notice is not an arrest warrant, and appearing does not surrender your rights. Take the notice's photo, note who issued it, and inform your lawyer before you go — better, have counsel accompany you or remain in contact through the visit. For persons apprehending arrest in a registered case, the sequence reverses: secure **pre-arrest bail first**, then join investigation from protection.

## Your rights during questioning

The two pillars: **no compulsion to self-incriminate** — a constitutional guarantee (Article 13) — and the evidentiary rule that renders **confessions to police inadmissible**: under the Qanun-e-Shahadat, a confession made to a police officer, or made in police custody outside a Magistrate's presence, cannot be proved against you. The signed "statement" pressed on people at police stations has, against its maker, essentially no evidentiary life — which is why the pressure to sign one is best met with polite refusal and a lawyer's presence.

You are entitled to counsel; to decline answering questions that incriminate you; to medical examination if mistreated (demand it at the earliest court production — injuries recorded late are injuries doubted); and to disclosure of the case number and allegation. Statements you do choose to make should be true, brief and consistent — investigators compare versions across sessions, and contradictions harvested in "informal chats" appear later dressed as case diary entries.

## Custody limits

An arrested person must be produced before a **Magistrate within 24 hours**. Further custody is either **physical remand** (police custody for investigation — granted for limited days, on justification, with courts required to apply mind, and opposed by counsel with your version and any mistreatment on record) or **judicial custody** (jail, outside police hands). Every remand hearing is an opportunity: it is where counsel contests custody, records complaints, and begins the bail clock's real work.

## Phones, devices and searches

Investigators routinely demand phones. Understand the layers: devices may be formally seized as case property against memos; the practical world of "unlock it here" is murkier, and disclosure under compulsion engages the same self-incrimination protections. Counsel's involvement disciplines the process — memos, witnesses to seizure, and challenge to fishing expeditions. Never delete data after a case begins: destruction reads as guilt and may itself be an offence; the same objective data that worries people frequently *acquits* them.

## Conduct rules that decide outcomes

Arrive with counsel informed and documents organised. Be punctual and unfailingly civil — the IO's file notes your demeanour, and courts read those files. Answer what is asked, briefly; volunteer nothing beyond your prepared, true account. Sign nothing without reading and without counsel's clearance — and keep copies of anything you do sign. Do not attempt hospitality, gifts or "mithai" — it converts you from suspect to briber. Afterwards, write down everything while fresh: times, officers, questions, statements — the contemporaneous note is counsel's raw material for everything that follows.

Investigation is where cases are actually won and lost in Pakistan — trials largely audit what investigation produced. Treat every interaction as on-the-record advocacy, conducted calmly, through counsel, with paper. That posture protects the innocent far better than either defiance or surrender.
