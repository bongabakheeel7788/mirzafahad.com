People arrive at a lawyer's office with two opposite misconceptions about wills: some believe a will can distribute everything as the writer wishes; others believe wills are meaningless for Muslims. Both are wrong. The will (wasiyyat) has a defined, limited, useful role in Pakistani inheritance — and using it well requires knowing its two boundary rules.

## The two rules that frame every Muslim will

**The one-third ceiling.** A Muslim may bequeath at most **one-third of the net estate** (after funeral expenses and debts). A bequest exceeding one-third is not void; it operates up to one-third and needs the heirs' consent — given after the death — for the excess.

**No bequest to an heir.** A bequest in favour of a person who is an heir at the time of death (a son, a widow, a daughter) is ineffective without the consent of the other heirs after the death. The will is an instrument for benefitting **non-heirs**: an orphaned grandchild otherwise excluded, a dependent relative outside the heir list, a faithful employee, a charity, a mosque or school.

Everything else — two-thirds or more of the estate — devolves by the fixed shares, and no testamentary language alters that.

## What a will is genuinely useful for

- **Orphaned grandchildren.** Where section 4 of the MFLO does not resolve a family's situation (or where doubt exists), a will within the one-third is the classical and safest provision for a predeceased child's children.
- **Charity and religious bequests**, specified precisely.
- **Non-heir dependents** — a widowed daughter-in-law, a foster child, long-serving staff.
- **Appointing an executor** to gather assets, pay debts and manage distribution — undervalued in Pakistan, where estates drift for years for want of anyone with authority.
- **Recording the estate** — a schedule of properties, accounts, debts owed and owing, spares heirs the archaeology that consumes the first year of most successions.
- **Funeral wishes and acknowledgment of debts** — including unpaid dower (a debt against the estate senior to distribution), and amanat (property held in trust for others) which should be identified so it is not distributed as the estate's.

## Form and proof

No particular form is prescribed: a will may be written or even oral, and needs no registration. But since wills operate exactly when their maker cannot testify, **provable form is everything**: a written, dated document, signed in the presence of two disinterested witnesses who also sign, ideally registered or notarised, with a medical note of soundness of mind where age or illness might later be alleged. Keep it where the executor can find it; a perfect will nobody finds achieves nothing.

A will is revocable at any time — by a later will, by destruction, or by dealing with the property inconsistently (selling the bequeathed house revokes that bequest by implication). The last valid will speaks.

## The recurring failure

The commonest Pakistani "will" is a document leaving everything to one or some children — often the sons, sometimes the caretaker child. As a will, it fails against the two rules above: heirs cannot be preferred by bequest without the others' consent. Families wishing to prefer someone must act **in life** — by valid gift with possession — with all the scrutiny that entails, or secure the genuine post-death consent of the other heirs. A paper will "cutting out" a daughter accomplishes nothing except revealing intent.

Used within its lane — one-third, non-heirs, executorship, clarity — a will is a modest document that prevents immodest amounts of conflict. Every adult with property should spend the one hour it takes.
