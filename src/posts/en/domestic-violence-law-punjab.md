Punjab legislated against domestic violence in 2016 with an Act whose architecture is genuinely protective — orders that restrain abusers, secure residence, and compensate — wrapped around implementation that varies sharply by district. Using it effectively means knowing both the orders and the practical channels that produce them.

## The Act's frame

The Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act, 2016 covers **violence in domestic relationships broadly defined** — physical abuse, and the Act's reach extends across the abuse spectrum including economic dimensions — committed against a woman by persons in a domestic relationship. Its philosophy departs from ordinary criminal law: rather than only punishing past acts, it builds **forward-looking protection** through court orders, on the family-court side of the system, with its own complaint routes and the district machinery (protection committees and, where operational, the violence-against-women center model piloted in Punjab) designed to receive and channel cases.

## The three orders

**Protection orders**: restraining the respondent from committing violence, communicating, stalking, or approaching the aggrieved — the core instrument, whose **breach is itself an offence** carrying imprisonment, which converts future violence from a fresh case to an immediate arrest matter.

**Residence orders**: the answer to the eviction threat that keeps women in violent homes — securing her right to reside in the household, restraining her dispossession, or arranging alternative accommodation where staying is unsafe. The order protects possession and safety; it does not transfer ownership.

**Monetary orders**: expenses and losses flowing from the violence — medical treatment, moved accommodation — ordered against the respondent, alongside the maintenance rights that exist independently in family law (our maintenance article covers that parallel track).

Interim orders are available while the case proceeds — in urgent situations, protection does not wait for full trial.

## The criminal law alongside

The Act runs parallel to, not instead of, the Penal Code: injuries are offences under the hurt provisions on the qisas/diyat framework, and the 2021 reforms addressing violence against women strengthened the criminal side's machinery. The practical implication: **medico-legal examination** at a government hospital after injuries — the MLC — serves both tracks, and is the single most important evidentiary step a victim or her family can take; injuries undocumented are injuries deniable. FIRs for serious violence proceed on the criminal track (with our FIR-refusal article's remedies where police stall), while the Act's orders secure the future.

## The practical channels

Immediate danger: police, and Punjab's **1043** women's helpline for guidance and routing. Shelter: **Dar-ul-Aman** through the court or district administration — imperfect institutions, but real refuge with legal process attached. Documentation: the MLC as above, photographs, dated messages and threats preserved, and the pattern log this series recommends in every abuse context. Filing: complaints under the Act go through the court machinery — counsel helps, and legal-aid channels (our legal-aid article) exist for women without means; khula, maintenance, dowry and custody claims can proceed in parallel in the family court, and frequently the combination — protection order plus family claims — is what converts a trapped situation into a negotiated, safe exit.

The Act's critics note its uneven implementation, and they are right. But its orders, where sought, issue and bind — and an abuser served with a protection order whose breach means arrest stands, for the first time, inside a legal boundary. Drawing that boundary is the point.
