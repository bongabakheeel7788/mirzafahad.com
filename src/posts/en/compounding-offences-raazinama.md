"Raazinama ho gaya hai" — the settlement is done — is how a large share of Pakistani criminal cases end. But compromise in criminal law is not a private transaction: which offences can be settled, with whom, and with what court involvement is fixed by law, and settlements done wrong have a way of coming back to life.

## The legal frame

Section 345 Cr.P.C. contains the tables of **compoundable offences**: for some, the named victim may compound directly; for others — the more serious — compounding requires the **court's permission**. Offences outside the tables are **non-compoundable**: no private settlement can end them, because the state, not the victim, is the offended party.

Broadly: many offences against person and property between private parties — hurt varieties, criminal trespass, mischief, defamation, adultery-adjacent private offences, and the qisas/diyat family discussed below — are compoundable in one mode or the other. Offences like acts against the state, corruption, narcotics, zina prosecutions under the Hudood framework, and most offences "against society" are not. Section 489-F (cheque dishonour) occupies its own well-known niche: treated as compoundable with the court's imprimatur, and in practice overwhelmingly resolved by payment-and-compromise.

## Qisas and diyat: the Islamic architecture

For offences against the human body — hurt through to qatl — the PPC's qisas and diyat provisions make the **legal heirs of the victim** the holders of the right, entitled to waive (afw) or compound against **badal-i-sulh/diyat** (compensation, whose value the government notifies annually against silver). Key consequences: **every legal heir** must join — an omitted heir (a widow, a minor whose share wasn't secured) can unravel the compromise; **minors' shares** require guardianship-court protection, with their diyat portions secured, not signed away by uncles; and in murder cases, even a complete compromise does not always end everything — where the court finds the case attracts **fasad-fil-arz** or a tazir residue, punishment can survive the settlement. Courts examine these compromises personally: heirs are identified from record, appear, and affirm the settlement's voluntariness.

## Procedure in practice

A raazinama is reduced to writing, thumb-marked/signed by the entitled parties with witnesses, and presented through counsel with an application: under 345(2) for court-permission offences, at bail stage, trial stage or even appeal (compounding is possible at any stage, including after conviction, with leave). The court verifies identity and voluntariness — women heirs are questioned directly to guard against family coercion — and, on acceptance, the accused is acquitted of the compounded offence; compounding has the **effect of an acquittal**.

Where an offence is non-compoundable, parties sometimes present "no objection" affidavits hoping for practical leniency; courts may weigh reconciliation at sentencing, but the case itself proceeds — promises otherwise are worth nothing.

## Drafting settlements that hold

The recurring failures are drafting failures: settlements that miss an heir; that leave payment "to be made later" without security (pay before or simultaneously with the compromise's acceptance, or secure it by guarantee — an acquitted accused's motivation to pay evaporates); that ignore the **civil side** (withdraw the connected civil suits expressly, or they continue); that forget **cross-cases** (compound both FIRs together, or the other side's case survives yours); and that humiliate — face-saving clauses cost nothing and keep village settlements settled.

## A final word to both sides

For complainants: a compromise trades your best leverage for compensation and peace — price it once, completely, and collect before signing. For the accused: a raazinama is an acquittal, not an apology on file — but pay what you promised; a revived enmity with a spent compromise is the worst of both worlds. And for both: do it through the court, on the record, every signature verified. The settlements that last are the ones a judge watched happen.
