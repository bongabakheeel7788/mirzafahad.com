The law is plain: when information discloses a cognizable offence, the SHO **must** register an FIR — section 154 Cr.P.C. leaves no discretion to weigh the complaint's truth at the registration stage. The practice, as every Pakistani knows, is otherwise: refusals, delays, "pehlay saalis kar lein", and pressure from the other side. The system provides a ladder of remedies, and using it in order is usually the fastest route.

## Step one: the written complaint and the senior officers

Deliver a **written** complaint at the police station and demand a receipt or diary number — oral visits leave no trace, and the paper trail matters at every later stage. On refusal, section 154(3) and standing police rules route you upward: an application to the **District Police Officer/SSP**, who can order registration or an inquiry. Many refusals dissolve at this stage, because the SHO's reluctance is local and the paper is now above him.

## Step two: the Justice of Peace — 22-A(6)

The signature Pakistani remedy: a petition under **section 22-A(6) Cr.P.C.** to the ex-officio **Justice of Peace** — the Sessions Judge/Additional Sessions Judge of the district. The petition states the offence, the approach to the police, and the refusal; the Justice of Peace calls a report from the SHO and, where the application discloses a cognizable offence, **directs registration of the FIR**.

Points that decide these petitions: attach your written complaint and its receipt (proof you actually approached the police); narrate a cognizable offence with dates, places and names — the Justice of Peace examines whether the *contents* disclose an offence, not whether they are ultimately true; and expect the other side's version, because counter-versions and cross-petitions are routine. Orders typically issue within weeks. Two sobrieties: the direction is to register, not to arrest — investigation follows its own course; and a 22-A order does not immunise a false complaint, which remains punishable.

## Step three: the private complaint — often the stronger road

Where police are captured by the other side, registration may win you an FIR and a hostile investigation. The alternative the law provides is the **direct complaint** under section 200 Cr.P.C.: you file the complaint before the Magistrate, your witnesses are examined, and the court itself summons the accused if a case is made out — police play no gatekeeping role at all. Complaint cases move on your diligence rather than the investigation's goodwill, and seasoned practitioners frequently prefer them precisely in influential-opponent situations. The two tracks can also coexist: an FIR case and a complaint case about one occurrence are managed together under settled procedure.

## Choosing your route

Time-sensitive evidence (medical examination in violence cases, CCTV) argues for pressing FIR registration fast — the FIR unlocks medico-legal process and police powers of seizure. Opponent-controlled police argue for the complaint route. Many matters do best with the sequence: written complaint, DPO application, 22-A petition — and a complaint case if investigation is strangled after registration.

## What not to do

Do not inflate the story to make the offence "more cognizable" — exaggeration discovered later guts the genuine core, and your first written version is the standard you will be judged against. Do not pay for registration; it marks the file and you. Do not broadcast the dispute on social media mid-process — it feeds defamation counter-suits and antagonises the very forums you are petitioning.

The refusal of an FIR feels like the system's door closing. In law it is one door of four — and the complainant who climbs the ladder with clean paper usually finds one open within the month.
