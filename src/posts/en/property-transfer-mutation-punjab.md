A large share of civil litigation in Punjab begins with a property purchase that went wrong. Most of it is preventable. This guide explains how title actually passes in Punjab and what to check before paying.

## Two systems: registration and revenue record

Punjab runs two parallel records for immovable property, and a buyer needs both to be in order.

**The registered deed.** Under the Registration Act, 1908, a sale of immovable property worth over Rs. 100 must be by a registered instrument — the *registry* or *bai'nama*, executed before the Sub-Registrar. This is the document that transfers ownership.

**The revenue record.** For agricultural land and much urban land in older areas, the ownership is also recorded in the *jamabandi* / *register haqdaran zamin* maintained by the Revenue Department (now largely through the Punjab Land Records Authority, PLRA). A change in this record is a **mutation** (*intiqal*). The mutation does not itself create title — the deed does — but a buyer whose name is not entered in the revenue record will face difficulty selling, mortgaging or defending possession later.

In housing societies and schemes (DHA, LDA, private societies), the equivalent of the revenue record is the society's own transfer register, and the transfer is done through the society office.

## The process in practice

1. **Obtain the fard.** The seller should provide a fresh *fard malkiat* (record of rights) from the Arazi Record Centre, dated within the last few days. This shows who the recorded owner is, the exact *khewat/khatooni/khasra* numbers, the area, and any recorded encumbrances.
2. **Verify the chain.** Ask how the seller acquired the property — by purchase, inheritance or gift — and see that document. If by inheritance, check that all legal heirs are recorded and that the seller has the share he claims. Sale by one heir of the whole property is a classic source of litigation.
3. **Check for encumbrances and disputes.** A pending suit, a bank charge, a prior agreement to sell (*bai'ana*), or an attachment may not appear on the face of the fard. A search at the Sub-Registrar's office and a check of the court record in the district are worth the cost.
4. **Agreement to sell.** For most transactions, an *iqrarnama* (agreement to sell) is signed first, with earnest money, fixing the price, the balance date and the consequences of default. This should be drafted carefully — it is the document you will sue on if the seller backs out.
5. **Execution and registration of the sale deed.** On the balance date, the sale deed is executed before the Sub-Registrar. Stamp duty, registration fee and (in urban areas) other levies are paid; the exact rates depend on the DC valuation table and the location. Both parties appear with CNICs and two witnesses. Biometric verification is now standard.
6. **Mutation.** The registered deed is presented to the revenue office (or through the PLRA centre), and the mutation is entered and attested by the Revenue Officer. Only after attestation does the buyer's name appear in the fard.
7. **Possession.** Take physical possession on the day of the registry, and record it in the deed.

## The frauds to watch for

- **Forged or duplicate fard.** Always obtain the fard yourself from the Arazi Record Centre, not from the seller.
- **Sale by a person who is not the owner.** Power of attorney sales are a frequent vehicle. Verify that the attorney is genuine, registered and not revoked, and that the principal is alive.
- **Undisclosed heirs.** Property recorded in a deceased person's name must first be mutated to *all* heirs by an inheritance mutation. A sale by some heirs binds only their shares.
- **Double sale.** A seller signs an agreement with two buyers. The buyer who registers first and takes possession usually prevails. Register promptly.
- **Encroached or disputed possession.** Visit the property, speak to neighbours, and confirm that the person in possession is the seller.

## If something has already gone wrong

The remedies depend on the stage:

- Seller refusing to complete after an agreement: suit for **specific performance** under the Specific Relief Act, 1877, with an injunction to stop him selling elsewhere.
- A deed obtained by fraud or from a person without title: suit for **declaration and cancellation** of the instrument.
- Wrong entries in the revenue record: application for **correction of mutation**, or a civil suit, depending on the error.
- Dispossession: suit for **possession**, and in some cases a complaint under the Illegal Dispossession Act, 2005.

Limitation periods apply to each of these and some are short. A purchaser who suspects a problem should seek advice at once rather than waiting to see whether the seller "comes around".

## Cost of getting it right

A proper title check, a well-drafted agreement and attendance at registration cost a small fraction of the price of the property. A suit for specific performance in Punjab can take years. The arithmetic favours doing the work before paying.
