Between the drama of an FIR and the quiet of a verdict lies a long, structured corridor that most families walk blind, misled by hearsay at every turn. Knowing the stages — what each one decides and what it cannot — converts panic into strategy. Here is the corridor, stage by stage, as it runs under the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898.

## Investigation and the challan

The FIR under section 154 CrPC only opens an investigation; it decides nothing. The police record statements (section 161), collect recoveries and medical and forensic reports, arrest or seek the accused, and conclude with the **report under section 173 CrPC — the challan** — naming who should face trial and on what evidence. The law expects the challan within a short statutory window (fourteen days as the norm), but interim challans followed by complete ones are everyday practice. An accused found innocent during investigation can be placed in the exculpatory column, though the court is not bound by the police opinion either way.

## Cognizance, supply of documents and the charge

The court takes cognizance and the accused receives, free of cost, copies of the entire prosecution record — FIR, statements, reports — in reasonable time before the trial opens. Scrutinise this bundle line by line with counsel; the trial will be fought from it.

Next the court frames the **charge**: the precise accusation, with offence, time, place and particulars. In Sessions cases this proceeds under Chapter XXII-A (sections 265-A onwards); in magistrates' trials under the parallel provisions of Chapter XX. If the accused pleads guilty the court may convict, but in serious offences courts almost always proceed to full trial regardless. An erroneous or defective charge can be amended, and material defects matter on appeal — so objections to the charge are taken then and there.

## Prosecution evidence — the heart of the trial

The prosecution produces its witnesses one by one: examination-in-chief, then **cross-examination** by the defence, then re-examination. This is where cases are won and lost — contradictions with 161 statements, improvements, hostile witnesses, the medical evidence against the ocular account, the timing of the FIR. Documents and recoveries are exhibited through the witnesses who prove them. The complainant's side cannot keep the file open forever; courts close evidence after fair opportunity.

## The section 342 examination

When prosecution evidence closes, the court examines the accused under **section 342 CrPC**. Every incriminating piece of evidence is put to him — "the witness says you fired; what do you say?" — and he answers without oath and without cross-examination. Two rules give this stage real teeth: circumstances **not put** to the accused under 342 cannot be used against him in the judgment, and his explanations, if reasonably possible, must be weighed. The accused may also opt, under **section 340(2) CrPC**, to testify **on oath** as a witness in his own defence — a serious tactical decision, since it invites cross-examination.

## Defence evidence and arguments

The defence may produce witnesses and documents, or produce nothing at all: the burden of proving the charge **beyond reasonable doubt** never shifts from the prosecution. In practice, defence evidence is led where there is an alibi, a counter-version, or documents the prosecution ignored. Then come **final arguments** — the prosecution and defence addressing the evidence against the law.

## Judgment and sentence

The court acquits or convicts by a reasoned judgment. One benefit of doubt — a single genuine, reasonable doubt — entitles the accused to acquittal; this is not a concession but the standard. On conviction, the court hears the convict on **quantum of sentence** and may, where law allows, consider probation for deserving first-time offenders. Appeal rights then open — to the Sessions Court or High Court depending on the forum — with their own strict limitation periods.

## Walking the corridor well

- Engage counsel at investigation stage, not after the challan: bail, the exculpatory column and evidence preservation are all decided early.
- Attend every date; absconding converts a defensible case into proclamation and warrants.
- Keep your own file of every order and statement copy.
- Do not negotiate with witnesses — tampering is itself an offence and destroys the defence it tries to help.

Every case deviates somewhere from the standard corridor — a quashing petition here, a compromise in compoundable offences there. A brief consultation with the FIR, the challan papers and the current order sheet will map exactly where your case stands and what the next stage can realistically deliver.
