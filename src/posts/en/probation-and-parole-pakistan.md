Not every conviction needs to end in a cell. Pakistani law carries two distinct second-chance mechanisms that courts and families chronically under-use: **probation**, which keeps a deserving offender out of jail at the sentencing stage, and **parole**, which brings a well-behaved prisoner out before his sentence ends. They are often confused; they operate at opposite ends of the sentence and through different authorities.

## Probation: the court's option instead of jail

The **Probation of Offenders Ordinance 1960** gives sentencing courts three graduated tools:

- **Conditional discharge (section 4):** for minor offences, the court may discharge the offender after admonition, or on a bond to be of good behaviour, without recording a conviction's usual consequences in full force.
- **Probation order (section 5):** the court, instead of sentencing, releases the offender on a bond — with or without sureties — to keep the peace and be of good behaviour for a period that may extend to **three years**, under the supervision of a **probation officer**.
- **Conditions:** the order can include residence requirements, abstention from intoxicants, restrictions on company, reporting duties and compensation to the victim as the court directs.

**Who qualifies** is the key filter. Under section 5, a **male** offender qualifies where the offence is **not punishable with death or imprisonment for life**; a **female** offender qualifies for any offence **not punishable with death**. Beyond the statutory floor, courts weigh age, character, antecedents, health and the circumstances of the offence — the provision is aimed squarely at **first-time offenders**, young offenders, and those whose offence was situational rather than professional. A probation officer's pre-sentence report commonly informs the decision.

The mechanics matter for defence lawyers and families: probation must usually be **asked for** — pressed at the sentence hearing with material showing clean record (police verification, character witnesses, employment or student status). Trial courts have the power; they exercise it far more readily when the groundwork is laid.

**Breach** has teeth. If the probationer violates a condition or commits a fresh offence, the court can forfeit the bond and **sentence him for the original offence** — the mercy was conditional, and the original file never died.

## Parole: early release from a running sentence

Parole operates on prisoners already serving sentences, under the **Good Conduct Prisoners Probational Release Act 1926** and its rules as applied in Punjab. The essentials:

1. **It is an executive decision, not a court order.** Release is granted by the provincial government (the Home Department machinery) on the recommendation of jail authorities and the parole/reclamation staff.
2. **Good conduct is the currency.** A prisoner whose jail record shows discipline and industry, who has served the qualifying portion of his sentence under the rules, and whose offence and background fit the policy, may be released **on licence**.
3. **Supervision continues.** The parolee lives and works at an approved place — historically with approved employers or community arrangements — under the watch of a **parole officer**, with conditions on movement and conduct.
4. **The licence is revocable.** Breach of conditions returns the parolee to prison to serve the remainder; time on parole is regulated by the licence's terms.

Parole is a privilege structured by rules and policy — categories of offence, portion of sentence served, victim and community considerations — not a right to demand. Its cousin, **remission**, shortens the sentence itself under jail rules and government orders, while parole changes **where and how** the remaining sentence is lived.

## Why these mechanisms are under-used

Partly information: families assume a conviction means jail, period, and never place probation material before the court. Partly machinery: probation and parole staff are stretched, and supervision capacity shapes how many orders are realistic. But appellate courts in Pakistan have repeatedly reminded trial judges that probation exists precisely so that first-time and young offenders are not sent to be schooled in prisons. In the right case — a young first offender in a compoundable or moderate offence, a woman convicted of a non-capital offence, a short sentence serving no one — a well-documented probation request is among the most valuable applications defence counsel can file.

Whether probation is available in a pending case, or parole is realistic for a family member already inside, depends on the exact offence, record and stage. A brief consultation with the judgment or jail warrant and the conduct record usually answers both questions — and produces the application or representation that moves the file.
