Pakistani law on women's inheritance is unambiguous: a daughter, a sister, a widow, a mother — each takes her fixed share, and no custom, family pressure or paperwork ritual lawfully removes it. The gap is not in the law but in enforcement, and the last decade has narrowed even that gap. This article names the standard methods of deprivation and the remedy for each.

## How deprivation actually happens

**The omitted mutation.** The brothers process the inheritance mutation without listing the sisters, banking on distance, ignorance or reluctance to "fight with family". Remedy: the omitted heir is still an owner in law. A suit for declaration and possession of her share — supported by the family tree and the record — succeeds, and superior courts have consistently refused to let limitation shelter this fraud: time runs from knowledge, and a co-heir's possession is treated as possession on behalf of all.

**The relinquishment (tanazul).** A sister is induced — at a funeral, at a wedding, "for the family's honour" — to sign or thumb-print papers surrendering her share, usually for nothing. Courts examine relinquishments by women with declared suspicion: want of consideration, want of independent advice, illiteracy or seclusion of the signatory, and the beneficiary's control of the process each cut against the document. Such releases are set aside regularly. Remedy: a suit to cancel the instrument and recover the share, brought as soon as its true nature is known.

**"She was given her share in marriage."** Dowry, wedding gold and wedding expenses are legally irrelevant to inheritance. This argument has no standing in court and should have none in negotiation.

**The benami and gift maneuvers.** Property moved before death into a brother's name, or an alleged oral gift surfacing after it. These are contested on the gift's essential elements and the fiduciary-burden principles discussed across inheritance case law: transactions benefitting those who controlled the deceased's affairs must be *proved* by them to be genuine.

**Attrition.** The most effective historic method: making the process so long and bitter that women abandon it. This is where the legal landscape has genuinely shifted.

## The modern enforcement tools

The **Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act, 2020** (in Punjab and at federal level) created a direct route: a deprived woman may complain to the **Ombudsperson**, who can investigate summarily and — where ownership is clear from the record — order restoration of possession without the woman first surviving a decade of civil litigation. It is designed precisely for the sister whose recorded share is being withheld by relatives in possession.

Alongside it: NADRA's succession system requires **biometric verification of every heir**, making silent omission harder; benami transactions carry their own statutory consequences; and criminal law reaches the forgery and fraud that these schemes involve.

## Practical course of action

First, get the record: the deceased's fard, the inheritance mutation, and NADRA's family certificate — a lawyer obtains these in days, and they show exactly what was recorded and when. Second, choose the forum that fits: Ombudsperson where the record is clean and possession is the issue; civil suit where documents (releases, gifts, mutations) must be cancelled; both are sometimes pursued in sequence. Third, move on actual knowledge — the law forgives late discovery, not late action after discovery. And a note to families acting in good faith: paying sisters the *value* of their share by genuine, documented agreement is lawful and common; the law's target is deprivation, not settlement. The difference is consent, consideration and paper that would survive a judge's reading.
