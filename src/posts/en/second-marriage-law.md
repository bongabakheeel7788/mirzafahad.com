Pakistani law does not prohibit a Muslim man from contracting a second marriage — but it regulates it, and the regulation has real teeth. Most men who find themselves prosecuted after a second marriage are not punished for the marriage itself but for skipping the legal procedure. Here is what the law requires.

## The permission requirement

Under section 6 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961, a man who wishes to marry during the subsistence of an existing marriage must apply in writing to the **Arbitration Council** (through the Union Council chairman), stating the reasons for the proposed marriage and whether the consent of the existing wife has been obtained. The application requires the existing wife's stance to be considered: the Council summons representatives of both spouses and grants or refuses permission.

Whether the first wife's *consent* is strictly indispensable, or whether the Council can permit despite her refusal for sufficient reason, is a question the forms and the case law have both engaged — but the practical position is simple: **without the Council's written permission, the second marriage triggers penalties.**

## What happens without permission

The second marriage remains **valid as a marriage** — the second wife is a wife, her children are legitimate. But the husband faces consequences:

- **Criminal liability**: prosecution on the first wife's complaint, punishable with imprisonment and fine under the Ordinance.
- **Immediate dower**: the entire dower (mehr) of the existing wife, prompt and deferred, becomes **immediately payable**. For a man who agreed a heavy deferred mehr assuming it would never fall due, this alone can be ruinous.
- **The first wife's right to divorce**: a nikahnama that delegated the right of divorce, or the ground of second marriage without permission, gives the first wife a clean route to dissolution — without returning dower, since dissolution on this statutory ground is not khula.

## The nikah registrar's role

A nikah registrar is required to satisfy himself about the permission before registering a second marriage; registrations obtained by misstatement (declaring oneself unmarried) expose both the groom and complicit persons to further liability, and false declarations are prosecutable in themselves.

## The second wife's position

The second wife — often the least-informed party in these events — retains her full rights: dower, maintenance, inheritance, and legitimacy of children, regardless of whether the Council's permission existed. A woman discovering after marriage that her husband concealed an existing wife has both the ordinary matrimonial remedies and the leverage of the criminal provisions.

## Practical guidance

For a man considering a second marriage: the procedure is not decorative. File the section 6 application, engage honestly with the Arbitration Council, and obtain the permission certificate before the nikah. The process is unromantic; the alternative is a criminal complaint, an accelerated dower debt, and litigation on two fronts.

For a first wife: your remedies are the criminal complaint, immediate recovery of your entire dower, maintenance, and — where your nikahnama or the facts support it — dissolution on favourable terms. Which combination serves you depends on what you actually want: leverage, separation, or financial security. Decide that first with your lawyer; the legal tools then line up behind it.

For the record: none of this applies to a man marrying after valid divorce or widowhood — the Ordinance regulates polygamy, not remarriage.
