A father dies leaving a bank account, some savings certificates and the family house. The bank will not release a rupee to the widow or children without "the court document" — and the family discovers there are two different documents with confusingly similar jobs, plus a newer NADRA route that may or may not apply to them. Choosing correctly at the start saves months.

## Two documents, two jobs

Both come from the Succession Act 1925, and both exist because institutions holding a deceased person's assets need legal cover before paying anyone.

A **succession certificate** covers **debts and securities**: bank balances, savings certificates, prize bonds, shares, insurance claims, unpaid salary and pension arrears. It certifies who the heirs are and authorises them to collect specified assets. It is the right tool when the estate's problem is essentially "money is sitting in institutions that need authority to release it."

**Letters of administration** go further: they appoint an **administrator** over the estate as a whole, with authority to collect all assets, pay the deceased's debts and distribute what remains among the heirs. They fit estates needing actual administration — multiple asset types, liabilities to settle, an heir abroad, assets whose holders demand letters specifically. Where the deceased left a **valid will** (effective, for Muslims, within the one-third limit and the rules on heirs' consent), the parallel document is **probate**, which confirms the executor named in the will.

Note what neither document does: **immovable property does not need either**. Land and houses pass to heirs by operation of law at death; the revenue record is corrected through **inheritance mutation (intiqal-e-wirasat)** at the land record centre, and this series covers that process separately.

## The NADRA route: the fast lane for agreed families

Since the Letters of Administration and Succession Certificates Act 2021, NADRA issues both documents through its Succession Facilitation Units for deaths where the heirs are in agreement. The shape of the process:

1. Any legal heir applies with the deceased's death certificate, CNICs of heirs and details of the assets.
2. NADRA generates the **family registration data** to identify the legal heirs from its records.
3. **All heirs verify biometrically** and consent; NADRA publishes a notice inviting objections.
4. Absent objection, the certificate or letters issue — typically within weeks, not the year-plus a contested court matter can take.

The route's limits define it: it works only where NADRA's records capture the family correctly and **every heir consents**. A disputed will, a disagreement among heirs, a question about who the heirs even are (a second marriage, an orphaned grandchild's claim), or an objection filed against the notice pushes the matter to court. Minors among the heirs are accommodated through their guardians, but complications around guardianship itself belong before a judge.

## The court route: where disputes and complications go

Where NADRA cannot act, the application goes to the **civil court of the district where the deceased ordinarily resided** (or where the assets are). The petition names the heirs and assets; the court issues notice to the heirs and a public citation; objections are heard; evidence of relationship is led where contested; and the court may require a **surety bond** before handing one heir authority to collect assets on behalf of all. Contested matters become, in substance, a trial of who the legal heirs are and in what shares — which is why a clean Family Registration Certificate from NADRA and the nikahnama, birth and death records are worth assembling before filing.

Court fee on these petitions is tied to the value of the assets covered, so the asset schedule should be accurate — neither inflated nor missing the account someone forgot.

## Practical sequencing for a grieving family

First, obtain the **death certificate** from the union council promptly and get NADRA's record updated. Second, inventory the assets: bank letters can be written asking what the deceased held. Third, choose the track — NADRA if the heirs align and records are clean; court if anything is contested or unusual. Fourth, handle immovable property separately through inheritance mutation. And throughout: an heir pressured to sign away shares "to simplify the paperwork" should pause — relinquishments signed in mourning are a recurring source of litigation for the next decade, especially against widows and daughters.

Which document, which forum and in what order is a half-hour's analysis with the death certificate, the family's CNICs and a list of assets on the table — settled best before the first application is filed, not after the wrong one bounces.
