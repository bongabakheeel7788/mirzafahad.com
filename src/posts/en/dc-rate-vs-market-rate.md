Ask what a property is "worth" in Pakistan and you will get three different numbers depending on who is asking: the market's, the province's, and the federal tax authority's. Every buyer and seller navigates all three, and misunderstanding them causes overpaid taxes, under-declared deeds, and disputes that surface years later.

## The three values

**Market value** — what a willing buyer actually pays a willing seller. No table records it; agreements do.

**DC rate (district collector's valuation table)** — the provincial government's area-wise minimum valuation, revised periodically for each mauza and urban locality. Its function: **stamp duty and provincial transfer levies** are charged on the higher of the declared price or the DC value. It exists precisely because parties under-declare; the table sets the floor the province will tax regardless.

**FBR value** — the Federal Board of Revenue's own valuation tables for major cities, applied for **federal taxes** on property transactions: advance income tax on purchase and sale under sections 236C/236K, and computation of capital gains. Where FBR has notified values, federal taxes apply on the higher of declared consideration or FBR value.

The three routinely diverge — market above FBR, FBR above DC, and all three moving at different speeds after each revision.

## What this means at the registry

At sale-deed registration, costs stack up against these floors: **stamp duty** and related provincial charges on the DC-or-declared value; **advance income tax** for buyer and seller against the FBR-or-declared value (rates differing sharply for tax filers and non-filers — the filer/non-filer gap is now one of the largest single costs in property deals); and local fees. Your total transaction cost therefore depends on the interplay of the declared price with both tables and both parties' filer status — a computation worth doing *before* signing the agreement, since the agreement should also say **who pays what**.

## The under-declaration trap

Declaring a lower price to save duty is endemic — and quietly dangerous, in ways sellers and buyers each discover later. For the **buyer**: the deed's declared price is his recorded cost; on resale, his taxable gain is computed from it, so today's stamp saving becomes tomorrow's inflated capital gain, and a declared value below FBR tables invites tax notices on the difference. For the **seller**: receiving heavy cash above the declared price creates unexplained money problems. For **both**: in litigation — pre-emption at the paper price, specific performance, cancellation — the declared figure takes on a life of its own, and the party who signed a false number argues uphill. The direction of policy (rising FBR values, filer/non-filer differentials, banking-channel requirements) is squarely against the cash-heavy under-declared deal.

## Practical guidance

Before any transaction: pull the current DC table entry for the mauza/locality and the FBR value where applicable, and compute the full tax stack both ways with your lawyer or tax adviser. Become a **filer** before transacting — the rate difference usually dwarfs the compliance effort. Declare defensibly; route payment through banking channels matching the deed. Sellers holding property across tax-law changes should have their capital-gains position checked against current holding-period rules *before* fixing a price, not after. And keep every deed, receipt and challan of this transaction — they are the cost record that protects you in the next one.

The three-price system is not going away; it is tightening. The parties who treat valuation as a compliance question at the planning stage keep the money that the parties who treat it as a signing-day surprise hand over in penalties.
