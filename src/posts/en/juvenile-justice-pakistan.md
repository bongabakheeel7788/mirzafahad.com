A police call about your son or a nephew is the kind of news that makes families act on panic — paying touts, hiding the child, or accepting whatever age the police wrote in the FIR. Pakistani law actually gives an accused child a separate, markedly gentler track: the Juvenile Justice System Act 2018. Knowing its four or five load-bearing rules in the first forty-eight hours changes the entire case.

## Who is a juvenile

The Act defines a juvenile as a person who has not completed eighteen years of age **at the time of the offence**. That cut-off matters more than anything else in the file. If the accused was 17 years and 11 months on the date of occurrence, every protection of the Act applies, even if the trial runs past his twentieth birthday. The first task of the defence is therefore fixing the age on the record — and doing it early, because an age plea raised at the appeal stage is received with suspicion.

## Age determination

Police routinely record ages by guesswork, and an entry of "19/20 years" in the FIR is not evidence of anything. When the accused claims juvenility, the court is bound to conduct an age inquiry. The hierarchy in practice:

1. **Birth certificate** issued by the union council — in Tandlianwala and similar tehsils this is the single most decisive document.
2. **NADRA record** — the B-form (child registration certificate) or the family's CRC showing the child's date of birth.
3. **School record** — the admission register entry of the first school attended.
4. **Medical examination** — an ossification test by a medical board, ordered only where documentary evidence is unavailable. Medical age estimates carry a margin of error, and courts extend the benefit of that margin to the accused.

Parents should gather these documents on day one, not after months of judicial custody.

## Juvenile courts and how trial differs

Juveniles are tried by a **juvenile court** — in most districts a sessions or magistrate's court notified to exercise juvenile jurisdiction — and the Act requires the trial to conclude within six months. The differences from an ordinary criminal trial are substantial:

- A juvenile cannot be tried together with an adult co-accused; the cases are separated.
- The child cannot be handcuffed or fettered (except in narrow circumstances for older juveniles posing escape risk).
- Proceedings are not open to the public, and publishing the juvenile's identity is prohibited.
- The court takes a report from a probation officer about the child's circumstances.
- A juvenile who cannot afford counsel is entitled to a lawyer **at state expense**.
- No juvenile can be sentenced to death, and ordinary imprisonment is displaced in favour of custody in a juvenile rehabilitation centre where confinement is unavoidable.

## Bail: the three categories

The Act grades offences by their maximum punishment, and bail follows the grade. For **minor offences** (up to three years), bail is a statutory right, and release can even be without surety. For **major offences** (three to seven years), bail remains the clear rule. For **heinous offences** (above seven years or punishable with death), a juvenile under sixteen still has a strong claim to bail, while an older juvenile's bail falls to judicial discretion — but even there, the principle that detention of a child is the last resort tilts the scales. A further safety valve: a juvenile kept in continued detention without the trial concluding can seek release on that delay alone, subject to the Act's conditions.

## Diversion: closing the case without a conviction

For minor offences — and major offences with consent — the Act allows **diversion** through a Juvenile Justice Committee: an apology, restitution, community service or similar disposition that ends the matter without a criminal conviction attaching to the child's record. Families routinely accept private "settlements" brokered at the police station that give no legal protection; a diversion order under the Act achieves the same peace with an actual legal effect.

## What parents should do immediately

- Do not let the child sign or thumb-mark anything at the police station.
- Secure the birth certificate, B-form and school record the same day.
- Insist that the age be placed before the magistrate at the first production.
- Engage counsel promptly — and remember that the Act itself entitles the child to free legal aid if the family cannot pay.

Every juvenile case turns on two early moves: proving age with documents and choosing the bail or diversion route that fits the offence category. A brief consultation with the FIR, the birth record and the school record in hand usually settles the whole strategy in one sitting.
