Losing a civil case is rarely the end of the road, but the road forks three ways — appeal, revision and review — and choosing the wrong fork wastes limitation, money and sometimes the case itself. The three remedies go to different courts, run on different clocks, and can change different things. Here is the map.

## Appeal — the full re-hearing

An appeal under **section 96 CPC** is the widest remedy: the appellate court re-examines both facts and law, can re-appraise the evidence, and can affirm, reverse or modify the decree, or remand the case. Where the first appeal goes depends on the suit's valuation: below the threshold fixed for Punjab it lies to the **District Court**, above it to the **Lahore High Court**. A second appeal to the High Court under **section 100 CPC** is narrower — substantial questions of law, not a third round on facts.

Limitation under the Limitation Act 1908 is generally **30 days** for an appeal to the District Court and **90 days** to the High Court, with the time genuinely spent obtaining certified copies of the judgment and decree excluded. Apply for copies the day the judgment is announced; the copy clerk's delay is excluded, yours is not.

An appeal can also carry an application to **suspend the decree's operation** — essential where execution (possession, recovery, demolition) would make the appeal pointless.

## Revision — the jurisdiction check

Revision under **section 115 CPC** is not a disguised appeal. It targets three defects only: the lower court **exercised jurisdiction it did not have**, **failed to exercise jurisdiction it did have**, or **acted illegally or with material irregularity** in exercising it. A finding of fact you dislike, or even a plainly generous reading of evidence, is not revisable; a court deciding a suit it had no power to hear, refusing to decide what it must, or trampling a mandatory procedure, is.

In Punjab the revisional forum splits by value: the **District Court** hears revisions against subordinate courts in smaller-value cases, the **High Court** in the rest. Limitation is **90 days**. Revision is typically the route against interlocutory orders that are not appealable — but courts discourage using it to stall proceedings, and costs follow frivolous revisions.

## Review — the same court, narrow grounds

Review under **section 114 and Order XLVII CPC** asks the **same court** to reconsider its own judgment. The grounds are deliberately narrow:

- **Discovery of new and important evidence** which, despite due diligence, was not within your knowledge or could not be produced earlier;
- **An error apparent on the face of the record** — something obvious without long argument, like a statutory provision overlooked or a calculation botched;
- **Any other sufficient reason**, read restrictively by the courts.

Review is not a chance to re-argue what was argued and lost. Limitation is short — commonly 20 to 90 days depending on the court — so treat it as an immediate-action remedy. A review is also generally barred once you have filed an appeal against the same judgment.

## Choosing between them

A practical sequence most lawyers run through:

1. **Is the order a decree or an appealable order?** If yes, appeal — it is the strongest remedy and usually excludes the others.
2. **No appeal available, but a jurisdictional or procedural illegality?** Revision.
3. **A glaring mistake the same judge would accept, or genuinely new evidence?** Review — often the fastest and cheapest where it fits.

The remedies also interact: filing one can close another, and limitation for the better remedy keeps running while you try the wrong one. Section 5 of the Limitation Act condones delay only for sufficient cause shown for each day — "my previous lawyer advised wrongly" succeeds sometimes, "we were deciding what to do" almost never.

Which fork is right turns entirely on what the judgment got wrong and which court said it. A brief consultation with the certified copy of the judgment and decree — ordered the day of announcement — settles the forum, the ground and the deadline in one sitting.
