Small employers in Pakistan tend to run on oral employment — until the first dispute: a manager who leaves with the customer list, a worker claiming years of unpaid dues, an EOBI notice with penalties attached. Employment law here is a patchwork, but its practical demands on an SME reduce to a manageable set.

## The appointment letter — the keystone

The industrial-relations framework (the standing-orders law in Punjab and its federal counterparts) obliges employers to issue an **appointment letter**, and every prudent employer should regardless of technical coverage: name, position, wage and allowances, hours, leave, probation, notice period, duties, and grounds and process for termination. In every employment case — dues, dismissal, competition — the tribunal's first question is "where is the appointment letter?", and the party with paper starts ahead.

Probation should be expressed (the standard framework contemplates probation of three months); confirmation, extensions, and the different termination rules during probation should be written rather than assumed.

## Wages, records and the floor

Provincial **minimum wage** notifications bind and update; pay at or above them and keep proof of payment — wage registers and bank transfers, because "he was paid in cash" persuades no forum. The Payment of Wages framework penalises delayed and unauthorised deductions; fines and recoveries have process requirements. Overtime, weekly holidays and leave entitlements flow from the shops-and-establishments and factories legislation applicable to your setup — a one-time compliance mapping with counsel is inexpensive and settles what actually applies to a shop, a restaurant, or a small factory respectively.

## Termination: the part everyone gets wrong

For covered workmen, termination is not at will. The framework distinguishes **termination simpliciter** (with notice — one month's notice or wages in lieu, for permanent workers) from **dismissal for misconduct**, which requires due process: a **charge sheet**, an opportunity to explain, a domestic **inquiry** observing fairness, and a proportionate order. Skipping the inquiry is the classic fatal error — labour courts reinstate or compensate on procedure alone, whatever the misconduct's truth. Keep the discipline file: warnings, absences, complaints, inquiry record.

On separation for any reason, clear **full and final dues** — wages, leave encashment, and where applicable gratuity/provident entitlements — against a signed receipt and settlement.

## The registrations SMEs ignore at their cost

**EOBI** (federal pension scheme) and provincial **social security (PESSI)** registration attach to establishments above modest employee thresholds, with monthly contributions per worker. Non-registration accumulates years of arrears, surcharges and personal exposure — the notices arrive eventually, usually triggered by a disgruntled ex-employee's complaint. Register when the law's thresholds are met; the contributions are small against the arrears trap, and the coverage (pensions, medical care through social security) is a genuine retention tool for staff.

## Protecting the business side

Confidentiality and non-solicitation clauses (customers, staff, recipes, supplier terms) are enforceable when reasonably framed; blanket "never work in this industry" covenants against ordinary employees fare poorly — restrain what is legitimately yours (data, clientele, trade secrets) rather than the person's livelihood. Company property, passwords and accounts should be documented as such, with return-on-exit clauses. For key hires, notice periods and handover obligations, spelled out, prevent the overnight departure that hurts most.

## The one-page compliance program

Issue appointment letters to everyone, current staff included — backdating honesty into the file beats no file. Pay through bank; keep registers. Map your establishment's coverage (minimum wage, hours, EOBI, PESSI) once with counsel. Discipline by paper: warn, charge-sheet, inquire. Settle leavers formally. An SME that does these five things has removed perhaps ninety percent of its employment-law exposure — for roughly one day of effort per year.
