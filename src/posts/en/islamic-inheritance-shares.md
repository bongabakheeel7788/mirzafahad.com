When a person dies in Pakistan, their property does not pass by choice or by custom — it passes by law. For Muslims, the shares are fixed by Islamic law and enforced by the courts, and no family arrangement, verbal promise or "understanding" can override them. Yet inheritance disputes remain among the most common cases in our courts, usually because families do not know the shares or because someone hopes the others won't ask.

## The basic rule

Everything the deceased owned at death — land, house, shop, bank balance, vehicles, business — forms the estate. Before distribution, three things are paid out first: funeral expenses, the deceased's debts, and any valid bequest (wasiyyat), which can cover at most one-third of the estate and cannot be made in favour of an heir. What remains is divided among the legal heirs.

## The main shares

The most common household situations work out like this:

- **Widow:** one-eighth of the estate if there are children; one-fourth if there are none. If the deceased left more than one widow, they share this portion equally.
- **Husband** (when the wife dies): one-fourth if there are children; one-half if there are none.
- **Sons and daughters:** after the fixed sharers take their portions, the children take the residue, with each son receiving twice the share of each daughter.
- **Father of the deceased:** one-sixth when there are children.
- **Mother of the deceased:** one-sixth when there are children (or siblings); one-third of the residue in certain cases where there are none.

## A worked example

A man dies leaving a widow, two sons, one daughter, and property worth Rs. 8,000,000.

The widow takes one-eighth: Rs. 1,000,000. The remaining Rs. 7,000,000 goes to the children in the ratio 2:2:1 (two sons at two units each, one daughter at one unit — five units total). Each unit is Rs. 1,400,000. So each son receives Rs. 2,800,000 and the daughter Rs. 1,400,000.

Now the same estate, but the deceased's mother is also alive. She takes one-sixth (Rs. 1,333,333), the widow one-eighth (Rs. 1,000,000), and the children divide the remaining Rs. 5,666,667 in the same 2:2:1 ratio.

## Common misconceptions

**"The family home belongs to whoever lives in it."** No. Possession is not ownership. A brother living in the family house holds the other heirs' shares in trust, and they can claim partition at any time — there is no time limit that extinguishes an inheritance right by mere delay in a co-ownership situation.

**"She got her dowry, so she has no share."** Dowry and marriage expenses have no effect on a daughter's inheritance. Her share is fixed by law and survives any family arithmetic.

**"He said before dying that the shop is mine."** An oral gift claimed after death is the single most litigated inheritance issue. A gift (hiba) requires offer, acceptance and delivery of possession during the giver's lifetime, and courts examine death-bed claims with deep suspicion.

**"We did a family settlement, so it's final."** A genuine written family settlement, signed by all heirs with knowledge of their shares, can be valid. One signed by some heirs, or obtained from women told they were signing "formality papers", is challengeable.

## What to actually do when someone passes away

Obtain the death certificate from the Union Council, then a NADRA succession certificate or court succession certificate for movable assets, and an inheritance mutation (intiqal-e-wirasat) for land — in every case listing **all** legal heirs. Distribution follows the record, so the single most important step is making sure no heir is omitted at the record stage. An omitted heir discovered years later unravels every subsequent sale.

If you suspect your share has been recorded wrongly — or was never recorded — a lawyer can obtain the revenue record and the succession file and tell you within a day where you actually stand.
