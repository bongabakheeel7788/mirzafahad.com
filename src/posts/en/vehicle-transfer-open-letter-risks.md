In every vehicle bazaar from Faisalabad to Tandlianwala, cars and motorcycles change hands on an "open letter" — a signed, blank-dated transfer file that lets the vehicle be sold three more times without anyone visiting the Excise office. The practice survives because it is convenient for dealers. For the two people at the ends of the chain — the registered owner whose name never comes off, and the final buyer whose name never goes on — it quietly allocates all the risk.

## What the law says ownership is

Under the Motor Vehicles Ordinance 1965 and its rules, the person recorded in the registration record is the vehicle's owner for the legal system's purposes, and both parties to a sale are required to report the transfer to the registering authority within the prescribed time. The sale itself — price paid, possession delivered — is a private transaction; the **record** is what the state, the traffic camera, the tax system and the criminal investigator read. An open letter is, in essence, an agreement to leave the record wrong indefinitely.

## The seller's exposure: your name keeps working after the car is gone

Sell on an open letter and everything the vehicle does remains addressed to you:

- **E-challans** photograph the plate and issue to the registered owner. They accumulate, block token tax clearance, and can surface years later with penalties.
- **Token tax and FBR data.** The vehicle keeps appearing in your asset profile; for a filer, an undeclared "sold" car that officially remains yours is an annual nuisance in wealth statement reconciliation.
- **Crime.** If the vehicle is used in a robbery, a hit-and-run or smuggling, the first door the investigation knocks on is the registered owner's. You will likely clear yourself — with time, counsel and luck in how the investigating officer proceeds — but "I sold it on an open letter to a man whose number is off" is a weak opening statement.

A seller's minimum protection, failing actual transfer: a **written sale agreement and delivery receipt** with the buyer's CNIC copy, witnesses, date and vehicle particulars, plus a copy kept of the signed transfer documents — and a report to Excise of the sale where the buyer disappears without transferring.

## The buyer's exposure: paying for what you cannot prove is yours

The final buyer on an open letter holds a vehicle registered to a stranger several links up the chain. Consequences: you cannot renew or claim **insurance** cleanly; a **dispute or theft** leaves you proving ownership through a chain of informal receipts; the registered owner (or their heirs, after death) can create real trouble, innocently or otherwise; and if any earlier link involved a **stolen, leased or bank-financed vehicle**, your possession can evaporate — superior claims of a leasing company or the true owner beat your open file. The discount an open-letter vehicle carries is not a bargain; it is the market pricing the risk you are accepting.

## Checking before you buy

1. **MTMIS verification.** Punjab's online motor vehicle record (and the SMS service for registration checks) shows the recorded owner, chassis and engine numbers and tax status. Match it against the card and the seller's CNIC.
2. **Physical match.** Read the chassis and engine numbers on the metal, not just the documents; tampered numbers mean walk away.
3. **Chain documents.** If the seller is not the recorded owner, demand the complete documented chain — each sale agreement, each CNIC copy — and treat gaps as the warning they are.
4. **Encumbrance.** An HPA (hire-purchase) endorsement in the record means a bank or leasing company's interest; insist on its removal before paying in full.

## Transferring is now the easy part

The historic excuse for open letters — a day wasted at the Excise office — has thinned. Punjab Excise supports online initiation of ownership transfer with biometric verification, and the traditional counter route remains: transfer application, both parties' CNICs, the registration file, payment of the transfer fee and any withholding tax (higher for non-filers; rates change, check current ones). Within days the record shows the new owner, and every risk described above switches off for both sides at once.

If you are sitting on either end of an old open-letter sale — a car sold years ago that still bears your name, or a vehicle you use but do not legally own — the exit is to reconstruct the chain and complete the transfer now, before a challan, a claim or an investigation forces the issue on worse terms. A short consultation with whatever documents exist usually reveals a workable path.
